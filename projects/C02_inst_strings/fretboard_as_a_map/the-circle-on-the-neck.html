The Circle of Fifths on the Neck

This file is the payoff bridge back to the Circle of Fifths. The claim:

The circle of fifths isn't just a poster you consult away from the guitar — a chunk of it is literally sitting across your open strings.

Once you see it, fifth-based harmony (which is most of it) becomes geometry on the neck.

1. Across the strings is the circle of fourths​

From the coordinate system, moving to the next higher string is +a perfect fourth (the G–B third aside). And a fourth up is the same move as a fifth down — going up a 4th and down a 5th land on the same pitch class (+5 ≡ −7 mod 12), which is exactly why the circle read one way is "fifths" and the other way "fourths" in the derivation.

So each open string is one step around the circle from its neighbor:

Open strings (low → high):   E → A → D → G   [ B ]  E
Each E→A→D→G step = up a 4th = one step COUNTERCLOCKWISE on the circle of fifths

Circle of fifths:  ... Bb  F  C  G  D  A  E  B  F# ...
Your bottom four strings:            └ G  D  A  E ┘  (read backward = E A D G)

Your four lowest open strings, E A D G, are four consecutive slots on the circle of fifths — the neck has the circle built into its tuning. (The B string's major third is the one break, then B→E resumes the fourths.)

2. Why this makes fifth-motion easy​

Most tonal harmony moves by fifths — ii–V–I, circle progressions, secondary dominants (all from the Circle of Fifths harmony files). On the neck, root motion by a fourth/fifth is often just jump to an adjacent string at a similar fret — a tiny hand move:

  • A root on the A string, its fifth-down (or fourth-up) neighbor sits right on the next string, roughly under the same finger. Walking the circle progression = walking across the strings.
  • ii–V–I root shapes fall on adjacent strings because their roots are circle neighbors — which is why the same two or three grips transpose all over jazz tunes.
  • Quartal voicings and drop-2 chords sit naturally, because stacking by fourths is the open-string interval.

The guitar rewards fifth/fourth-based thinking geometrically — the physical layout and the harmonic map are the same shape.

3. Octaves and navigation shapes​

Two more bits of the neck's geometry make theory concrete:

  • The octave shape. Because the axes have fixed steps, the same pitch class an octave up sits at a repeatable offset (e.g. up 2 strings and +2 frets on the lower strings). Learn one octave shape and you can find any note by leapfrog, without naming every fret.
  • CAGED is just five interlocking snapshots of these movable shapes around the neck — bookkeeping for the B-string kink, not a separate theory. It's a way to keep chord and scale shapes oriented as they move.

4. The neck is itself a chart​

Step back and the neck joins the family of projections from Circle of Fifths → The Chart Is a Projection. The fretboard is a 2-D map of pitch whose adjacencies were chosen to make transposition and fifth-motion cheap (uniform frets + fourths tuning), at the cost of a slightly irregular note-name layout. It surfaces exactly what a chordal, transposing instrument queries most — and hides absolute note names, which is why guitarists think in shapes.

5. Takeaway​

  • Adjacent strings differ by a fourth = one step on the circle of fifths, so E A D G are four consecutive circle slots — the circle is in your tuning.
  • This makes fifth-based harmony geometric: circle progressions and ii–V–I roots fall on adjacent strings, so the shapes transpose everywhere.
  • Octave shapes and CAGED are navigation tools over that same geometry.
  • The fretboard is itself a projection/chart, optimized for transposition and fifth-motion — the same lens as the whole Circle of Fifths series.

That's physics → temperament → free pitch → the neck as theory, all reading back to the one idea that a map is a compromise built for the questions you ask most.

One map is left: the one in the player's head. If the fretboard is an index optimized for transposition, what does that do to how a guitarist thinks — and how does it differ from a pianist? That's Mindsets: Guitarist vs Pianist.
