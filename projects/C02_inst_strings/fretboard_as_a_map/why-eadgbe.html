Why EADGBE

Standard guitar tuning is E A D G B E — four perfect fourths, then a major third, then a fourth. It looks arbitrary and it trips up every beginner. It isn't arbitrary: it's the classic engineering compromise between two things you can't fully have at once — a symmetric, easy-to-navigate neck and a chord-friendly one. This file is why the odd third earns its place.

1. The tempting alternative: all fourths​

Tune every string a perfect fourth above the last (E A D G C F) and the coordinate grid becomes perfectly uniform — +5 semitones across every string boundary, no exceptions. The payoff is real:

  • Every shape is identical anywhere on the neck, in both directions.
  • Scales and arpeggios have one pattern, no B-string kink to memorize.
  • The mental model collapses to a single, regular tiling.

Jazz players (famously Stanley Jordan; also Allan Holdsworth-adjacent thinking) use all-fourths for exactly this reason. So why isn't it standard?

2. The cost of all fourths: chords get hard​

Guitar is, above all, a chordal instrument — six strings meant to be strummed together — and all-fourths tuning is bad at chords. A basic triad or a rich seventh voicing ends up spread too far apart to grab: pure fourths stack the strings so that the notes you need for common open chords fall outside a comfortable four-fret hand span. You gain a tidy grid and lose the ability to easily play the very thing the instrument is for.

3. What the major third buys​

Dropping the B string a semitone (from the all-fourths C to B) inserts a major third between G and B. That one adjustment:

  • Pulls the top strings' pitches closer together, so triads and seventh voicings land inside a four-fret reach — you can actually fret them.
  • Enables the whole vocabulary of open-position chords (the open E, A, D, G, C shapes) and the standard barre chords derived from them.
  • Gives the familiar, resonant open-string chords that define how the guitar sounds.

The price is the broken across-string axis: shapes kink at the B string, and you carry a little mental bookkeeping (the CAGED shifts). Standard tuning spends symmetry to buy chord-reachability — and for a strummed, chordal instrument, that was the right trade. That's why it "won."

4. Alternative tunings = re-optimizing for a different query​

Once you see standard tuning as one point in a trade-off, alternate tunings stop being exotic — each retunes the neck to make a different task cheap, at the cost of others:

TuningMakes cheapPays with
All fourths (EADGCF)uniform shapes, scale symmetryhard chords
Open G/D/E (open-chord tunings)one chord rings on open strings; slide guitarother chords/keys awkward
DADGADmodal drones, folk fingerstylestandard chord shapes don't transfer
Drop Done-finger power chords, low Done shifted string to relearn

This is the same logic as the Circle of Fifths → Build Your Own Chart: a tuning is an index over the fretboard, optimized for the queries a player runs most. Standard EADGBE is simply the best general-purpose index for a chordal instrument — good at everything, perfect at nothing.

5. Takeaway​

  • EADGBE is a compromise: four fourths would make a perfectly symmetric, shape-uniform neck, but chords would be unreachable.
  • The G–B major third shrinks the top-string spacing so triads, sevenths, open chords, and barre chords fit under the hand — paid for with the B-string kink.
  • Alternate tunings re-optimize the neck for specific tasks (slide, drones, power chords); standard tuning is the best all-round index for a chordal instrument.
