The Mix — Running Both Models

The two blind spots from the last file are not symmetric flaws to accept. They're each other's fix. The guitarist is missing exactly what the pianist has too much of, and vice versa. The goal of this file: run both indexes over the same music and switch between them on purpose.

A mindset is a projection, not an identity. You can learn a second one.

Note what this is not: it isn't "learn the other instrument." You don't need a piano to think like a pianist. You need to start asking the questions the other model answers.

1. What a guitarist should steal from the pianist​

Name what you play. The cheapest high-value habit: whenever you land a shape, say the actual notes out loud once. E-shape barre at fret 4 → "G♯, D♯, G♯, C, D♯, G♯." Do it slowly, a handful of chords at a time. This bolts the missing identity layer onto shapes you already own — you're not learning new music, you're labeling old music.

Track one voice through a progression. Play a ii–V–I and follow only the third — where does it go, does it move by a semitone, does it stay put? This is functional harmony made audible, and it's the single fastest way to stop hearing chords as blocks.

Learn the neck by absolute landmarks. Not shapes: names. All the Cs on the neck, then all the F♯s. Ten minutes, twelve pitch classes, and the two-second pause disappears.

Play in a key, not at a fret. Deliberately play the same tune at three different neck positions in the same key. This decouples "key" from "fret," which is the confusion the shape model quietly installs.

2. What a pianist should steal from the guitarist​

Transpose by pattern, not by re-derivation. Take a short phrase and move it up in semitones through all twelve keys, thinking of it as the same shape moved rather than twelve separate keys. The keyboard fights you — that's the point; the fight is where the relative model gets built.

Think in intervals from the bass. Instead of "E♭, G, B♭, D," think "root, +4, +3, +4." Interval-first is the guitarist's native tongue and it makes voicings portable across keys and instruments.

Walk the circle physically. Play a circle progression and feel it as motion — a consistent direction of travel — rather than a list of chord names. Same content, relational encoding.

Meet the compromise directly. Sit in on a piano tuning once, or just detune two notes slightly and listen to the beats. The stretch curve and inharmonicity stop being theory the moment you hear them. Better still: play the same interval on a fretless instrument and hear what pure actually sounds like — the whole point of Just Intonation in Practice.

3. The mix already exists in hardware​

Instrument builders have been trying to merge these models for a century, and looking at their attempts sharpens what the trade actually is:

LayoutWhat it doesWhich mindset it enables
Jankó keyboardkeyboard rebuilt as a uniform whole-tone gridone shape per chord in all keys — piano range, guitar geometry
Button accordion / bayanisomorphic button gridfingerings transpose by sliding, like frets
Linnstrument / Continuumgrid controller, all-fourths tuning, continuous pitchmovable shapes plus bends and microtones
All-fourths guitarremoves the G–B kink (why it exists)maximally uniform grid, harder chords
DAW piano rollkeyboard laid flat as a time × pitch gridabsolute pitch view for people who never touch a piano

Two things stand out. First, isomorphic layouts win the transposition argument decisively — and they still haven't displaced the piano, because a century of repertoire, technique, and teaching is welded to the standard keyboard. Layouts persist for the same reason EADGBE did: switching cost, not optimality.

Second, the piano roll is quietly the most successful mindset export in music. Anyone producing in a DAW is reading pitch off a keyboard axis — absolute, unique, one place per note — regardless of what instrument they play. Producers who came up on guitar end up bilingual whether they intended to or not.

4. When to switch which model on​

Running both means knowing which one to reach for:

Composing / analyzing harmony      → pianist model (names, voices, function)
Improvising, transposing on stage  → guitarist model (shapes, distances)
Learning a new tune                → pianist model to understand it,
                                     guitarist model to move it
Talking to another musician        → whichever index THEY are running

That last line is the practical payoff of this whole folder. "Move it up two" and "so, F♯ minor?" are the same instruction in two languages, and the person who can speak both is the one the band stops arguing around.

5. Takeaway​

  • The two mindsets are each other's fix: guitarists need identity (names, voices, notation); pianists need relationship (shapes, intervals, transposition).
  • You don't need the other instrument — you need to ask the other model's questions: name your shapes; transpose by pattern.
  • Isomorphic layouts (Jankó, button accordion, Linnstrument) are the hybrid built in hardware; the DAW piano roll is the pianist's model exported to everyone.
  • A mindset is a projection you can change, not a fact about who you are.

That closes the String Instruments section as it stands: physics → temperament → free pitch → the neck as theory → the player's own map. Every layer is the same idea — a map is a compromise built for the questions you ask most, and the last map in the chain is the one in your head.
