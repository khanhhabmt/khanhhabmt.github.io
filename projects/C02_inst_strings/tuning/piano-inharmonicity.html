Piano Inharmonicity & Stretch Tuning

Equal temperament (previous file) fixes the "twelve keys must close" problem, but it quietly assumes something false: that a string's overtones are exact whole-number multiples of its fundamental. Piano strings are thick, stiff, and under huge tension — and that stiffness breaks the assumption badly enough that tuning a piano to mathematically correct frequencies makes it sound out of tune.

The result: pianos are deliberately tuned away from equal temperament, by far more than the comma we've been fussing over.

1. Real strings are stiff, and stiffness sharpens the overtones​

The clean harmonic series (1f, 2f, 3f, 4f, …) assumes an ideal string — thin and perfectly flexible. A real piano string resists bending, and that stiffness pushes every overtone progressively sharp:

Ideal string:   f,  2f,     3f,     4f,     5f, ...      (whole-number multiples)
Piano string:   f,  2.003f, 3.01f,  4.02f,  ...          (each partial creeps sharp)

The standard model:

f_n ≈ n · f · √(1 + B · n²)

where B is the inharmonicity coefficient of that particular string. B grows with stiffness — thicker, shorter strings have larger B. Two consequences right away:

  • Short pianos suffer more. A spinet or small upright has short bass strings with big B; a 9-foot concert grand has long strings with small B. This is a real acoustic reason (not just marketing) that longer pianos sound "cleaner."
  • The higher the partial, the worse the drift — the n² term means upper partials are pulled sharp fastest.

2. Why a "perfect" octave beats​

Here's the trap. Tune two notes an octave apart to an exact 2:1 ratio of their fundamentals. Sounds correct — but the ear judges an octave by whether the lower note's 2nd partial lines up with the upper note's fundamental:

Lower note A2 — its 2nd partial:  should be 2·f, but inharmonicity pushes it SHARP
Upper note A3 — its fundamental:  sits at exactly 2·f
→ the two don't match → they beat → the octave sounds "dirty"

So a mathematically perfect 2:1 octave beats audibly on a real piano. The math is right; the piano still sounds wrong.

3. The fix: stretch tuning (the Railsback curve)​

Tuners solve this by stretching: tuning octaves so they're beatless against the actual (sharp) partials rather than against the theoretical 2:1. In practice that means:

  • Treble notes are tuned progressively sharp of equal temperament.
  • Bass notes are tuned progressively flat of equal temperament.

Plot the deviation from ET across the keyboard and you get the S-shaped Railsback curve:

At the extremes the deviation reaches roughly ±30–40 cents — nearly a third of a semitone, and far larger than the 23.46-cent comma that the whole temperament debate was about. A real piano departs from ET more, on purpose, than temperament ever asked it to.

4. Why no two pianos stretch the same​

Because B differs per string and per instrument, the ideal stretch curve is unique to each piano. This is why piano tuning resists full automation from a fixed frequency table: a good tuner (or software like Verituner / TuneLab / CyberTuner) first measures the inharmonicity profile of that specific piano, then computes a stretch curve tailored to it. Tuning a piano is fitting a model to the instrument in front of you, not applying a universal lookup.

5. The other factors that "still sound wrong"​

Inharmonicity is the headline, but a piano is a coupled physical system with more going on than frequency:

  • Unisons (2–3 strings per note). Most middle/treble notes have two or three strings that must be tuned to each other. A hair of deliberate detuning fattens the tone and lengthens sustain (a technique called chorusing); too much and it beats unpleasantly.
  • String coupling & false beats. Strings, bridge, and cast-iron frame resonate together; a single string can even beat against itself ("false beat") from tiny irregularities.
  • Weather. Tension, plus temperature and humidity, swell and shrink the wood and steel — so the piano drifts over days and seasons. That's why regular tuning is a maintenance schedule, not a one-time event.
  • Voicing. The hardness/shape of the hammer felt changes the balance of overtones — a matter of timbre, not pitch, but it's part of making a piano "sound in tune" to the ear.

6. Takeaway​

Equal temperament assumes ideal strings. Piano strings are stiff, so their overtones drift sharp (inharmonicity), which makes even a "perfect" octave beat. Pianos are therefore stretch-tuned — trebles sharp, basses flat, up to ±30–40¢ — and the exact stretch is unique to each instrument's B profile. On top of that sit unisons, coupling, weather, and voicing.

Guitar faces the same ideal-string lie, but wears its compromise even more openly — you can see it in the shape of the saddle. That's the next file.
