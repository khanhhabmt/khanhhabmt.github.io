The Comma & Temperament

This file is the bridge between pure acoustics and real instruments. It answers a question that trips up almost everyone the first time: if the perfect fifth is exactly 3:2, and that's a real, measurable physical ratio, then why does every piano and guitar tune its fifths slightly wrong?

The short version: because you cannot have both pure fifths and a closed, play-in-any-key system at the same time. Temperament is the name for the compromise you're forced to make.

1. The 3:2 fifth is real — and measurable three ways​

The perfect fifth isn't a convention; it's a physical constant you can measure without knowing any music theory:

  • String length — stop a string at 2/3 of its length and it sounds a fifth higher. (Frequency is inversely proportional to length, so 2/3 length ↔ 3/2 frequency.)
  • The harmonic series — a single vibrating string already contains the fifth: its 3rd harmonic over its 2nd harmonic is 3f / 2f = 3/2.
  • Beats — play two notes near a fifth apart and detune slightly; you hear a "wah-wah" beating. The beats vanish exactly when the ratio hits pure 3:2.

So 3:2 is not up for debate. The trouble starts when you try to build a whole instrument out of it.

2. Pure fifths can't close the circle​

This is the comma, derived in full in the Circle of Fifths → Why the Ring Closes. The one-line recap:

Twelve pure fifths:  (3/2)^12 = 531441 / 4096 ≈ 129.746
Seven octaves:       2^7      = 128
Mismatch (comma):    531441 / 524288 ≈ 1.0136       (a frequency ratio)
                     → 1200 × log2(1.0136) ≈ 23.46 cents

(Converting a frequency ratio to cents is always cents = 1200 × log2(ratio) — the 1200 because an octave is defined as 1200 cents, and the log2 because the ear hears pitch logarithmically. See the full derivation for the step-by-step.)

Stack twelve pure 3:2 fifths and you overshoot seven octaves by about a quarter of a semitone. A true spiral of pure fifths never returns home — so if you insist on pure fifths, your instrument can't play in all twelve keys with a fixed set of notes. The comma has to be dealt with.

3. "Temper" means "detune on purpose"​

Here's the vocabulary that clears up the confusion:

Tuning to exactly 3:2 is just intonation — it is not a temperament. Temperament begins the moment you deliberately leave 3:2 to absorb the comma somewhere.

The comma is a fixed debt of 23.46 cents. Every tuning system is just a different policy for where to hide it:

SystemFifthsWhere the comma goesCost
Just / Pythagoreanpure 3:2 (untempered)dumped on one unused "wolf" fifththat one fifth howls; can't modulate freely
Meantoneflattened hardspent purifying the thirds insteadstill a wolf; limited key range
Well temperamentunevenly adjustedspread unequally across keyseach key has its own "color/mood"
Equal temperament (ET)all flat by 1/12 comma (≈1.955 ¢)spread perfectly evenlyevery key identical; nothing is pure, everything plays

Modern Western instruments run on equal temperament: every fifth is shrunk to 2^(7/12) ≈ 1.4983 instead of 1.5, so twelve of them close exactly onto seven octaves. You trade a tiny, imperceptible (~2¢) error on every fifth for the freedom to play in any key on a fixed keyboard or fretboard.

4. What a tuner actually targets​

A practical consequence worth internalizing: a standard tuner does not aim at the pure ratio. With A = 440 Hz, the note E a fifth above is:

Pure (just) fifth:        440 × 3/2      = 660.000 Hz
Equal-tempered fifth:     440 × 2^(7/12) = 659.255 Hz   ← what the tuner calls "in tune"

The gap between them is a ratio first, then converted to cents:

660 / 659.255 ≈ 1.00113           (frequency ratio)
→ 1200 × log2(1.00113) ≈ 1.955 cents

— which is exactly 1/12 of the comma. If you tune a beatless (pure) fifth by ear, an ET tuner will read it as ~2 cents sharp. The tuner is temperament, packaged into a device — it has already left 3:2 behind.

5. Where this is heading​

Everything so far still assumes ideal strings. Equal temperament solves the "twelve keys must close" problem, but it silently assumes each string's overtones are perfect whole-number multiples. Real strings break that assumption — and that's where the next two files pick up:

  • Piano (Inharmonicity & Stretch Tuning) — stiff strings whose overtones drift sharp, forcing tuners to stretch beyond ET.
  • Guitar (Frets & Compensation) — where ET is literally carved into the fretboard, and where fretting a real string sharpens it enough to need physical compensation.

Key takeaway: the 3:2 fifth is a real physical constant; temperament is the deliberate act of leaving it, because a fixed-pitch instrument can't have pure fifths and play in every key. Equal temperament is the modern compromise — but it's still built for an ideal string that doesn't exist.
