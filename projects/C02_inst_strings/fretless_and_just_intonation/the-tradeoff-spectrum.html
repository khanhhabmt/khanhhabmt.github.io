The Fixed-to-Free Spectrum

This file closes the loop that opened back in The Comma & Temperament. Every instrument in this whole section can be placed on one axis: how much its pitches are fixed in advance versus chosen live. Seeing the axis whole explains why both ends exist and neither "wins."

1. The axis​

FIXED / tempered ────────────────────────────────────────────→ FREE / expressive

piano · organ   fretted guitar   [microtonal /    fretless bass   trombone · voice
                                   adjustable       · violin
                                   frets]           family

pitches locked   locked to ET     grid, but a       continuous —    continuous —
to a tempered    by the frets,    denser or         pitch chosen    pitch chosen
grid by the      but compensated  custom one        by ear per      by ear /
builder          per string                          note           slide

Moving left → right, you gain expressive and pure intonation and lose the safety net — reliability, ease, and easy polyphony. It's a smooth trade, not a cliff.

2. What each end is actually optimizing​

The fixed end (piano, fretted guitar) optimizes reliability + polyphony + "any key." To play ten notes at once, repeatably, in every key, on an instrument one person can operate, you must pre-decide the pitches — and pre-deciding pitches for all keys forces a compromise, which is exactly temperament. Equal temperament is the price of a keyboard you can trust and play chords on. The fret is the same bargain in cheaper form: give up pure intonation and micro-expression, get an instrument that's in tune on day one and lets you barre a chord.

The free end (violin, voice, trombone) optimizes purity + expression. With one melodic line (or a small, listening ensemble), the ear can chase pure ratios and bend pitch for feeling — so there's no need to freeze a compromise into the instrument. The cost is everything the fixed end bought: years to play in tune, and hard polyphony.

3. Why the middle is thin​

Notice the bracketed middle — adjustable-fret and microtonal instruments — is sparsely populated. That's not an accident. The middle tries to have both and pays twice: it keeps a grid (so it's still quantized) but makes that grid denser or movable (so it loses the fretted instrument's simplicity). Solutions like True Temperament frets live here — clever, but niche, because they add complexity to buy back a purity most players don't miss enough to pay for.

4. The meta-point: a fret is a compromise chosen for a use​

Step back and this whole section rhymes with the Circle of Fifths → Build Your Own Chart idea. There is no "correct" instrument on the axis, only the compromise that fits a job:

  • Need to comp chords in any key, tonight, reliably? → fixed end. Take ET.
  • Need one singing line that locks pure into a quartet, or bends for feeling? → free end. Take the fingerboard and the years of ear training.

The fret, the tempered keyboard, the bare fingerboard — each is an index built for a query, exactly like a chart. Choosing an instrument is choosing which question you want to be easy.

5. Takeaway​

  • Every string instrument sits on one axis: pitch fixed in advance ↔ chosen live.
  • The fixed end buys reliability, polyphony, and all-key playability — and pays with temperament (frozen compromise).
  • The free end buys pure and expressive intonation — and pays with difficulty and hard polyphony.
  • The middle is thin because trying to get both costs the simplicity that made the fixed end worth it.
  • Like a chart, an instrument is a compromise optimized for a use — there's no universally right point on the axis.

With pitch understood from both ends — frozen (tuning) and free (this folder) — the last folder turns to the fretted neck itself and reads it as a coordinate system for theory.
