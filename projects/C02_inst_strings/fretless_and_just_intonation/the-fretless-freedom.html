The Fretless Freedom

The guitar frets file ended on a hard truth: a fretboard is equal temperament frozen into wood. Every note you can play is a position the builder already decided, locked to 2^(n/12). This section is about the opposite design choice — take the frets off — and what that trades away and buys.

1. Frets are a quantizer​

A fret turns a continuous string into discrete, pre-chosen pitches. Put your finger anywhere in a two-fret span and the note snaps to whichever fret you're behind — the string speaks from the fret, not your fingertip. In signal terms, a fretboard is a quantizer: it rounds your analog finger position to the nearest allowed (equal-tempered) value.

That quantizer is a feature — it's why fretted instruments are forgiving and fast to learn. But it means a guitar cannot play a pitch that isn't on the ET grid. The pure 3:2 fifth (660 Hz over 440, not 659.255) simply isn't reachable on a standard fret.

2. Remove the frets → pitch becomes analog​

Take the frets away and pitch goes continuous. Now the exact spot your finger lands sets the pitch, with infinite resolution. The whole fretless / free-pitch family works this way:

InstrumentHow pitch is chosen
Violin family (violin, viola, cello, double bass)bare fingerboard — finger position, no frets ever
Fretless bass / fretless guitarfretboard with the frets removed
Trombonea slide — a continuous length control
The human voicevocal-fold tension, fully analog

All of them share one property the guitar lacks: the player picks the pitch of every note, live, by ear.

3. The trade: freedom for a safety net​

This is a genuine engineering trade-off, not a free upgrade.

What you lose — the safety net.

  • No mechanical guide means intonation is entirely on the player's ear and muscle memory. Land a millimetre off and you're audibly flat or sharp.
  • It takes years to reliably play in tune. A beginner violinist sounds rough for exactly this reason, while a beginner guitarist is in tune (ET) on day one.
  • Fast, clean polyphony is hard: tuning one finger by ear is tough; tuning three or four simultaneously, in real time, is brutal.

What you gain — expression and purity.

  • Pure intonation. You can place a fifth at true 3:2, a major third at true 5:4, and make chords lock (the next file is all about this) — impossible on a tempered fret.
  • Expressive micro-tuning. Vibrato, portamento (sliding between notes), and bending a leading tone slightly high for tension are all continuous gestures built into the instrument, not add-ons.
  • Any tuning system, any microtonality — maqam, raga, and other traditions that live between the piano keys are native here.

4. The spectrum this opens up​

So the fret is really a UX decision on a slider between two extremes:

quantized / reliable  ←──────────────────────────→  continuous / expressive

  piano, organ        fretted guitar        fretless bass, violin, voice
  (fixed ET,          (fixed ET,            (any pitch, chosen live,
   builder's grid)     builder's grid)       by ear)

Fretted instruments (and keyboards) sit on the left: dependable, fast, polyphonic, but locked to the tempered grid. Free-pitch instruments sit on the right: capable of pure and expressive intonation, but demanding and hard to stack into chords.

The next file walks over to the right end and asks: when players can choose any pitch, what do they actually choose — and why does a string quartet in pure tune shimmer in a way a piano never does?

5. Takeaway​

  • A fret is a quantizer: it rounds finger position to a builder-chosen, equal-tempered pitch — forgiving, but locked off the pure ratios.
  • Removing frets makes pitch continuous, as in the violin family, fretless bass, trombone, and voice.
  • The trade is real: you give up the safety net (easy, reliable, polyphonic) to gain pure intonation and continuous expression.
