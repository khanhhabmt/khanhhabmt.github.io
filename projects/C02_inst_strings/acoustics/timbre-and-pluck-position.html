Timbre & Pluck Position

Two guitarists play the exact same note — same pitch, same tuning — and one sounds bright and nasal, the other round and warm. Nothing about the frequency changed. What changed is timbre: the recipe of how loud each harmonic is. And the single biggest thing a player controls, live, is where along the string they pluck.

1. Timbre is a weight vector over the harmonics​

From the previous file, a string sounds all its modes f, 2f, 3f, … at once. Timbre is just how much of each. For a data person, the cleanest mental model is Fourier:

sound(t) = a1·sin(2π·f·t) + a2·sin(2π·2f·t) + a3·sin(2π·3f·t) + ...
                └── the "timbre vector" [a1, a2, a3, ...] ──┘

Same pitch f for everyone; the amplitudes [a1, a2, a3, …] are what your ear reads as "bright / dark / hollow / nasal." Change the vector, change the tone — without touching a single frequency.

2. Plucking at a node kills that harmonic​

Here's the lever. Recall (file 01) that mode n has nodes — dead points — at every 1/n of the string. You cannot start a mode vibrating if you pluck it right at one of its nodes: plucking at a point suppresses every harmonic that has a node there.

Pluck at the MIDDLE (L/2):
   the midpoint is a NODE for every EVEN harmonic (2f, 4f, 6f...)
   → even harmonics are silenced
   → only odd harmonics remain → a hollow, clarinet-ish tone

The general rule: plucking at 1/k of the string weakens the k-th harmonic (and its multiples). Where you strike literally shapes the timbre vector.

3. The bright-to-warm axis you already use​

This is why the plucking hand roams:

bridge  ←─────────────────────────────────────────→  neck / soundhole

near the bridge          over the soundhole / middle
(pluck at a small        (pluck near L/2)
 fraction of L)
→ excites MANY high      → excites mostly the low
  harmonics                harmonics, kills evens
→ BRIGHT, thin, nasal    → WARM, round, mellow
  ("sul ponticello"       ("sul tasto" / "dolce")
   on bowed strings)
  • Rock/funk rhythm and aggressive tones → pick near the bridge for cut and bite.
  • Jazz comping, ballads → pick up near the neck for a fat, dark tone.

Same note, same amp — you're re-weighting the harmonic vector by moving a few centimetres. Bowed-string players have named positions for this (sul ponticello near the bridge, sul tasto over the fingerboard), because it's a core expressive control, not an accident.

4. The other timbre knobs (briefly)​

Pluck position is the headline, but the same "which harmonics, how loud" framing covers the rest:

  • Pick vs flesh vs nail. A hard, pointed pick excites more high harmonics (bright); a thumb or fingertip rounds them off (warm). It's the sharpness of the initial displacement — a sharper "corner" in the string's starting shape means more high-harmonic content.
  • Attack angle & force. Digging in harder doesn't just get louder; it briefly adds high harmonics (and, on real strings, a touch of pitch rise), which reads as "aggressive."
  • Decay is not static. High harmonics lose energy fastest (they bend the stiff string more per cycle), so a plucked note starts bright and darkens as it sustains. The timbre vector is a function of time, not a fixed snapshot — the "ping" of the attack mellowing into a rounder sustain.

5. Takeaway​

  • Timbre = the amplitude vector over the harmonics; pitch is fixed, tone is the mix.
  • Plucking at a node silences that harmonic, so plucking near the bridge (bright, high-harmonic-rich) vs over the soundhole (warm, fundamental-heavy) is your fastest tone control.
  • Pick hardness, attack, and time-varying decay all move the same vector.

So far the string has made a sound in the abstract. But a bare string barely moves any air — it's nearly silent. Turning that vibration into audible sound is the job of the body or the pickup, which is the next file.
