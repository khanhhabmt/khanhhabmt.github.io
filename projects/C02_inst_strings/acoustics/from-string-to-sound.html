From String to Sound

A vibrating string is, on its own, almost silent. It's too thin to push much air — it slices through it instead of moving it. Every string instrument is really a machine for solving that one problem: how do you turn a tiny, quiet string vibration into audible sound? There are two families of answer — a body, or a pickup — and they define the acoustic-vs-electric split.

1. The impedance-mismatch problem​

Think of it as a signal-transfer problem. The string carries lots of energy but in a form the air can't accept: it's a high-impedance source (small, fast, light) trying to drive a low-impedance load (the big, floppy mass of air). Couple them directly and almost nothing transfers — like a tiny gear trying to spin a huge one. You need a matching stage in between. That's what a soundboard (or a speaker cone) is: an impedance matcher that lets the string's energy actually reach the air.

2. The acoustic answer: body as amplifier and filter​

On an acoustic guitar, violin, or piano, the chain is:

string → bridge → soundboard (top) → air
                        ↑
                 body cavity resonance
  • The bridge transfers the string's vibration into the soundboard (the top). The top has far more area, so it moves real air — this is the main radiator. This is why the top wood and its bracing dominate an acoustic instrument's voice; back and sides matter much less.
  • The body cavity is a Helmholtz resonator — the springy air in the box plus the mass of air in the soundhole form a resonance (the same physics as blowing across a bottle). It's tuned low, boosting the bass and giving the instrument its "boom." So the body doesn't just amplify; it filters, coloring some frequencies more than others.
  • Tonewood, honestly. What matters most is the soundboard's stiffness-to-mass ratio and how it's braced — that sets which frequencies radiate efficiently. It's a real effect, but a far smaller and subtler one than marketing implies, and it lives almost entirely in the top.

A cost comes with this: every bit of energy the body radiates is energy leaving the string, so a loud acoustic tends to have shorter sustain. Radiation and sustain trade off.

3. The electric answer: sense the string directly​

A solidbody electric barely radiates at all (that's the point — no feedback-prone resonant box). Instead a magnetic pickup senses the string's motion electrically:

magnet + coil under a STEEL string
 → the vibrating steel string modulates the magnetic field
 → a changing field through the coil induces a voltage (Faraday's law)
 → that voltage is the signal sent to the amp

Two consequences worth knowing, both echoes of earlier physics:

  • Pickup position samples the string at one point — exactly like plucking position in the timbre file, but for listening instead of exciting. A pickup near the neck sits under a big antinode of the low harmonics → fat, warm. A pickup near the bridge sees mostly high-harmonic wiggle → bright, cutting. Same "which harmonics does this location see" logic.
  • Single-coil vs humbucker. A single coil is bright and detailed but acts as an antenna for mains hum. A humbucker is two coils wired so that hum (arriving equally in both) cancels, while the string signal (wired to add) survives — at the cost of some high end. It's noise cancellation by symmetry.

(Because the pickup needs a magnetic string, electrics use steel/nickel strings — nylon or bronze won't drive a magnetic pickup, which is why classical and acoustic strings differ.)

4. Coupling, sustain, and wolf tones​

Once string and body are coupled, they become one system, with a few emergent effects:

  • Sustain vs loudness — as above, more efficient radiation drains the string faster. Solidbody electrics sustain for ages partly because they radiate so little acoustically.
  • Wolf tones — when a played note lands right on a strong body resonance, the string and body fight each other and the note "warbles" or chokes. Cellos are notorious for this; players fit a small "wolf eliminator" mass to detune the offending resonance. It's the downside of a strongly resonant body.
  • Feedback — a hollow electric near a loud amp lets the speaker re-drive the body, which re-drives the string: a runaway loop. Solidbodies exist largely to break it.

5. Takeaway​

  • A bare string is an impedance mismatch with the air — nearly silent — so every instrument adds a matching stage.
  • Acoustic: the soundboard radiates and the body cavity (Helmholtz) filters/boosts the lows; the top wood and bracing dominate, and radiation trades off against sustain.
  • Electric: a magnetic pickup senses the steel string directly; its position samples harmonics just like pluck position does, and humbuckers cancel hum by symmetry.
  • Coupling brings sustain trade-offs, wolf tones, and feedback.

That closes the acoustics arc: string → harmonics → timbre → sound. Next we return to pitch — but from the opposite extreme of the fretboard: what happens when you take the frets off entirely, in Fretless & Just Intonation.
