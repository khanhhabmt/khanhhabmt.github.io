The Chart Is a Projection

Everything so far treated the circle of fifths as the map. It isn't. It's one projection of the 12-pitch-class space, chosen because it makes key distance and harmonic function cheap to read. Other charts project the same twelve notes differently to make other relationships cheap — and pay for it by hiding the ones the circle showed.

There is no master chart. Every diagram trades away some relationships to surface others. A chart is an index, and each index is optimized for a different query.

This file is a tour of the major alternative projections and, crucially, what query each one makes cheap and what it hides.

1. The chromatic circle: the same points, relabeled​

Arrange the twelve notes by semitone instead of by fifth and you get the chromatic circle. It has the identical twelve points — it's literally the circle of fifths multiplied by 7 (mod 12), since a fifth is 7 semitones:

Fifths order:     C  G  D  A  E  B  F# ...     (add 7 each step)
Chromatic order:  C  C# D  D# E  F  F# ...      (add 1 each step)
Map between them:  n  ↦  7n mod 12   (the "M7 / M5" multiplicative transform)

The two are isomorphic — same object, different embedding. But the reading changes completely:

  • Chromatic circle makes cheap: semitone voice-leading, and the symmetric scales — the augmented triad (every 4 steps), the diminished seventh (every 3), the whole-tone scale (every 2) all appear as regular polygons.
  • Chromatic circle hides: key relationships and function — adjacent keys are no longer adjacent points, so ii–V–I is not a neat local shape.

The circle of fifths hides exactly what the chromatic circle reveals, and vice versa. They are the two "prime" projections (7 and 5 are the coprime generators), and the M7 transform even lets you rewrite melodies by swapping one for the other.

2. The Tonnetz: a 2-D lattice for voice leading​

The Tonnetz ("tone network") drops the single ring and uses a 2-D grid: one axis moves by fifths, another by major thirds (a third implied). Triads become triangles; neighboring triangles share an edge (two common tones).

Its transformations are the neo-Riemannian operations, each flipping one note by a small interval to swap a triad for its neighbor:

P (Parallel):        C major ↔ C minor    (move the 3rd by a semitone)
L (Leading-tone):    C major ↔ E minor    (move the root down a semitone)
R (Relative):        C major ↔ A minor    (move the 5th up a whole tone)
  • Tonnetz makes cheap: maximally smooth voice leading — chains like P L P L... glide by single-semitone moves, which is how a lot of chromatic Romantic and film harmony actually works. It also reveals that pitch space is topologically a torus (both axes wrap).
  • Tonnetz hides: key signatures and diatonic function — it's built for triad-to-triad smoothness, not for "what key am I in."

The Tonnetz is the answer to a different query than the circle: not "where's home?" but "what's the least-motion neighbor of this chord?"

3. The Coltrane circle: symmetry by major thirds​

Divide the octave into three equal parts (major thirds) instead of walking fifths and you get the structure behind Coltrane's Giant Steps. Diagrams called "Coltrane circles" overlay this major-third symmetry on the wheel to expose three tonal centers a major third apart (e.g. B–G–Eb) cycling with ii–Vs between them.

  • Makes cheap: symmetric, equal-division key schemes (the augmented-axis moves that ordinary functional harmony treats as "distant").
  • Hides: ordinary tonal gravity — it's a chart for a specifically symmetric compositional idea, which is why it looks baroque to anyone who isn't running that particular query. (A perfect example of a chart that is most valuable to the person whose music actually uses it — the theme of file 09.)

4. Cognitive and algorithmic charts​

Two more worth knowing, because they come from measuring rather than deriving:

  • Krumhansl–Kessler key profiles / key-distance maps — built from listener experiments (which notes "fit" a key), yielding an empirical inter-key distance that, reassuringly, comes out looking a lot like the circle of fifths plus the relative-minor relationship. Query: "how related do keys actually sound to humans?"
  • Chew's Spiral Array — a 3-D helix that un-collapses the enharmonic seam (file 02): it refuses to glue F# and Gb, keeping the spiral open so an algorithm can track spelling and find keys geometrically. Query: "let a machine infer key/spelling from notes." Note this is the honest helix of file 01, deliberately left unclosed.

6. What to carry forward​

  • The circle of fifths is one projection among several, all of the same twelve pitch classes; the chromatic circle is even the same object under the M7 transform.
  • Each chart surfaces one class of relationship and hides another. Choosing a chart is choosing a query.
  • Which sets up the final question: if charts are query-specific indexes, then the "complex personal chart" you noticed is just an index overfit to one person's queries — and you can build your own, deliberately. That's file 09.
