Why the Ring Closes

The circle of fifths is drawn as a closed loop. That is a convenient lie — and understanding exactly which lie tells you almost everything about how Western tuning works. In reality the "circle" is a spiral (a helix) that we force shut by agreeing to detune every fifth by a hair. This file is the derivation from first principles.

1. The generator: the perfect fifth as a 3:2 ratio​

After the octave (2:1), the most consonant interval is the perfect fifth, whose frequencies stand in the ratio 3:2. This is not a cultural choice — it falls out of the harmonic series (the 3rd harmonic over the 2nd). If you're going to build a pitch system by stacking one interval, the fifth is the natural generator because it's the simplest ratio that isn't just octave duplication.

So the question becomes: what happens if I keep stacking 3:2 on top of itself?

2. The arithmetic that refuses to close​

Stack the fifth twelve times and you expect to land back on your starting note, seven octaves higher. Let's check whether the numbers actually agree.

Twelve fifths:   (3/2)^12 = 531441 / 4096   = 129.74634...
Seven octaves:   (2/1)^7  = 128             = 128.00000...

They do not match. The ratio between them is the famous mismatch:

Pythagorean comma = (3/2)^12 / 2^7
                  = 531441 / 524288
                  = 1.0136433...            (a frequency ratio)
                  → 1200 × log2(1.0136433)  (convert ratio → cents)
                  ≈ 23.46 cents

(The 1200 × log2(ratio) step is the standard way to turn a frequency ratio into cents; it's explained under point (c) below and used consistently throughout.)

Conclusion: a true stack of pure 3:2 fifths never returns home. Plotted in pitch space it's a helix that keeps climbing, never closing into a ring. The poster on the wall is lying to you by about 23 cents.

Reading that calculation, term by term​

That four-line block packs three separate ideas together. Here's each one on its own.

(a) Why those exponents. Pitch intervals multiply, they don't add. Going up one fifth multiplies frequency by 3/2; doing it twelve times is (3/2)^12. Going up one octave multiplies by 2; doing it seven times is 2^7. We compare these two specific towers because twelve fifths should, in principle, land on the same note as seven octaves: a fifth spans 7 semitones, so 12 × 7 = 84 semitones = 84 / 12 = 7 octaves. Both roads are supposed to arrive at the same place.

Twelve fifths → (3/2)^12 = 3^12 / 2^12 = 531441 / 4096 ≈ 129.746
Seven octaves → 2^7      = 128         = 524288 / 4096   (same denominator)

Notice 129.746 ≠ 128: the fifths road ends up slightly higher. That overshoot is why the ring can't truly close.

(b) Why we divide. The size of the gap between two frequencies is a ratio, not a difference. To ask "how far did the fifths overshoot the octaves?" we divide the two endpoints. Sharing the denominator 4096 makes the division clean:

comma = (3/2)^12 / 2^7 = 531441 / 524288 = 1.0136433...

1.0136 means the twelve-fifths note is 1.36% higher in frequency than the seven-octaves note. If the two roads agreed, this ratio would be exactly 1. It's a hair above 1, and that hair is the Pythagorean comma — the reason the "circle" is really the climbing helix above.

(c) Why that becomes ≈ 23.46 cents. A raw ratio like 1.0136 is hard to feel, so intervals are measured in cents, a unit defined (not derived) so that:

1 octave      = 1200 cents          ← a convention, chosen to divide nicely
1 octave      = 12 equal semitones
→ 1 semitone  = 1200 / 12 = 100 cents

The number 1200 was picked precisely so each equal-tempered semitone comes out to a round 100 cents. Because the ear hears pitch logarithmically (each doubling of frequency feels like the same step up), the conversion uses a log:

cents = 1200 × log2(ratio)
      = 1200 × log2(1.0136433)
      ≈ 23.46 cents

Now put 23.46 next to that ruler: one semitone is 100 cents, so

23.46 / 100 ≈ 0.235 ≈ 1/4 semitone

The comma is about a quarter of a piano key — nowhere near the next note, but far bigger than the ~5–10 cents the ear can detect. Small, unignorable, and (as the next section shows) something every tuning system has to hide somewhere.

3. How we force the ring shut: equal temperament​

If twelve pure fifths overshoot seven octaves by one comma, the fix is blunt: shrink every fifth by 1/12 of the comma (≈ 1.955 cents). Do that and the fifth becomes exactly:

Equal-tempered fifth = 2^(7/12) ≈ 1.49831   (ratio)
    → 1200 × log2(2^(7/12)) = 1200 × (7/12) = 700.000 cents
Pure (just) fifth    = 3/2 = 1.5            (ratio)
    → 1200 × log2(1.5)                     ≈ 701.955 cents
Difference           = 700.000 − 701.955    = -1.955 cents   (imperceptible melodically)

(Note the shortcut: log2(2^(7/12)) = 7/12 exactly, so the equal-tempered fifth lands on a clean 1200 × 7/12 = 700 cents — no calculator needed. Only the pure fifth, 1200 × log2(1.5), actually requires the logarithm.)

Now twelve fifths equal exactly seven octaves, the helix collapses onto itself, and the circle genuinely closes. The circle of fifths is a property of equal temperament, not of physics. We paid for a closed, modulate-anywhere system with a tiny, evenly-spread tuning error on every fifth.

The comma has to go somewhere. Every historical tuning is a different policy for hiding that 23-cent error:

  • Pythagorean — keep all fifths pure, dump the entire comma onto one unused fifth (the howling "wolf" fifth).
  • Meantone — flatten fifths hard to purify the thirds instead; still leaves a wolf.
  • Well temperament — distribute the comma unevenly, so each key has its own "color" (this is the world of Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier).
  • Equal temperament — spread it perfectly evenly; every key sounds identical, nothing is pure, everything is playable.

4. Why twelve, and why fifths, visit every note​

Here's the data-structure view. Measure everything in semitones and work mod 12 (pitch classes ignore octaves). A perfect fifth is 7 semitones. So "stack fifths" means repeatedly adding 7 in Z/12Z:

0 → 7 → 14≡2 → 9 → 16≡4 → 11 → 18≡6 → 1 → 8 → 3 → 10 → 5 → 0
C   G    D     A    E      B    F#/Gb  Db   Ab   Eb   Bb   F   C

This walk touches all twelve pitch classes before returning, in a single unbroken cycle. That is not luck — it happens because gcd(7, 12) = 1. The step size 7 is coprime to 12, so the generator 7 generates the whole group Z/12Z.

Contrast this with stepping by a whole tone (2 semitones): gcd(2, 12) = 2, so you'd get two disjoint six-note cycles (the two whole-tone scales), never one tour. The circle of fifths is "complete" precisely because 7 and 12 are coprime — the same reason a 12-hour clock hit by a 7-hour hand visits every hour.

Note also: a perfect fourth is 5 semitones, and gcd(5, 12) = 1 too. Going up by fourths is the same as going down by fifths (5 ≡ -7 mod 12) — which is why the circle read counterclockwise is the "circle of fourths." Same object, opposite orientation.

Where the twelve itself comes from. Everything above assumes a twelve-note universe and then shows the fifth tours it completely. That assumption has its own derivation, which is the subject of Scale Systems: stacking fifths and folding into an octave yields a clean two-step scale only at n = 5, 7, 12, and twelve turns out not to be a scale at all but the closure of the seven-note diatonic set under transposition. If you've wondered why this file is allowed to start at twelve, that section is the prequel.

5. What to carry forward​

  • The circle is a Z/12Z cycle generated by 7 — a modular-arithmetic object, closed only because equal temperament sanded off the Pythagorean comma.
  • Its closure is an engineering decision, not a natural law. Older tunings left the seam visible.
  • Because 7 is coprime to 12, ordering the pitches by fifths is a genuine reordering of all twelve notes — and, as the next file shows, that reordering turns out to be the sorted index of every key signature.
