Progressions as Walks Around the Ring

If root motion down a fifth is the strongest resolution (file 04), then the most natural progression imaginable is simply "keep resolving" — take one counterclockwise step, land, step again. That is the circle progression, and a surprising amount of tonal and jazz repertoire is some decorated version of it.

A chord progression is a walk on the circle. The classic ones are counterclockwise walks (chained fifth-drops); the colorful ones deliberately break the walk at chosen moments.

1. The circle progression: pure counterclockwise motion​

Chain fifth-drops through the diatonic chords of C major and you get:

I → IV → vii° → iii → vi → ii → V → I
C → F  → Bdim → Em  → Am  → Dm → G → C

Each arrow is one step counterclockwise (down a fifth) on the circle. This sequence — sometimes called the diatonic circle of fifths — is the harmonic skeleton behind countless pieces (Baroque sequences, Pachelbel-adjacent loops, "I Will Survive," "Fly Me to the Moon"). You rarely hear the entire eight-chord chain; you hear arcs cut out of it.

2. ii–V–I: the three-slot atom​

The most important arc is the last three chords: ii → V → I.

   ...  Dm  →  G  →  C
        ii     V     I

On the circle these are three adjacent slots, walked counterclockwise. It is the fundamental cadential unit of jazz and a huge fraction of common-practice music because it stacks both pulls from file 04: predominant ii sets up dominant V, whose tritone then resolves down a fifth to I. Learn to see any ii–V–I as "three neighbors, walked home," and you can transpose it anywhere on the wheel instantly.

Jazz tunes are often chains of ii–Vs targeting different tonics — e.g. "Autumn Leaves" is essentially two interlocking ii–V–Is (one major, its relative minor), which on the wheel is two overlapping three-slot walks sharing a spoke.

3. Pop's "four chords": a walk that leaves the neighborhood​

The ubiquitous I–V–vi–IV (the "four-chord song") is instructive because it is not a clean circle walk:

I → V → vi → IV
C → G → Am → F
  • I → V is one step clockwise (tension up).
  • V → vi is a deceptive move — the dominant fails to fall to I and lands on its relative vi instead (the "surprise" that keeps the loop from resolving).
  • vi → IV slides to the subdominant; IV → I (looping) is the gentle plagal return.

The catchiness comes precisely from the broken walk: the ear expects the circle's counterclockwise resolution, and the deceptive V→vi withholds it, looping energy instead of discharging it. Pop lives on withheld resolution; jazz lives on chained resolution. Same wheel, opposite reading strategies.

4. A taxonomy of root motions​

Any two adjacent chords in a progression move by one of a few root intervals. Reading them as circle-geometry tells you the feel before you hear it:

Root motionOn the circleTypical functionFeel
Down a 5th1 step counterclockwiseV→I, circle progressionstrong resolution
Up a 5th1 step clockwiseI→V, setupsrising tension
Down a 3rdinner-ring hopI→vi, I→IV?smooth, coloristic
Up a 3rdinner-ring hopI→iiilifting, gentle
Down a 2nd~5 steps acrossV→IV, backdoor-ishplateau / rock feel
Up a 2nd~5 steps acrossIV→V, bVII→Ienergetic push

Notice: strong, "classical" motion clusters near the center of the wheel (±1 step); "modal/rock" motion jumps across it (±5 steps, i.e. stepwise roots). That single observation predicts why rock progressions (I–bVII–IV) feel "flatter" and less goal-directed than jazz ii–V–I chains — the rock roots leap across the circle instead of walking its neighbors.

5. Sequences: repeat the walk, transpose the pattern​

A sequence is the same short walk applied over and over, each iteration shifted one slot. Because the circle is homogeneous (every slot has identical local geometry), a pattern that works at one position works at all of them — the descending-fifths sequence is literally "do the ii→V gesture, then rotate the whole thing one step counterclockwise, repeat." Sequences are the clearest proof that the circle is a symmetry group: the same move is available everywhere.

6. What to carry forward​

  • The circle progression (chained fifth-drops) is the backbone; real music uses arcs of it, above all the three-slot ii–V–I.
  • Broken walks create pop's charm (deceptive V→vi); chained walks create jazz's momentum.
  • Root-motion distance on the circle predicts feel: near-center (±1) = goal-directed and "classical"; across-the-wheel (±5, stepwise) = modal/rock and "plateau-like."
