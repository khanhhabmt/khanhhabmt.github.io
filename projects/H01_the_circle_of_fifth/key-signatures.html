The Sorted Index of Key Signatures

The single most useful reframe in this whole series:

The circle of fifths is not primarily a circle of notes. It's the sorted index of key signatures — ordered so that each neighbor differs from the last by exactly one accidental.

If file 01 explained why stacking fifths tours all twelve pitch classes, this file explains why that particular ordering is the useful one: it's a minimal-edit sort, a near-Gray-code over the twelve diatonic scales.

1. One step = one accidental​

Start at C major: zero sharps, zero flats. Move one step clockwise (up a fifth) to G major. What changed? Exactly one note: F became F#. Everything else is identical.

C major:  C  D  E  F   G  A  B
G major:  G  A  B  C   D  E  F#     ← only F → F# is new

Move again to D major and exactly one more note sharpens (C → C#). This is a theorem, not a coincidence: transposing a major scale up a perfect fifth changes precisely one pitch class — the old 4th degree rises a semitone to become the new 7th (the leading tone). In pitch-class-set terms, adjacent keys on the circle are at Hamming distance 1: they share six of their seven notes.

Going counterclockwise (down a fifth) is the mirror image: each step adds one flat. C → F flattens B → Bb, and so on.

So the circle is a Gray-code-like ordering of the twelve diatonic collections — consecutive entries are one edit apart. That "minimal edit between neighbors" property is the source of nearly every practical use later: closely-related keys, pivot chords, smooth modulation.

2. The order of sharps and flats is the circle​

Because each clockwise step adds one sharp, the order in which sharps appear is itself a walk of fifths:

Order of sharps:  F  C  G  D  A  E  B      (each a fifth above the last)
Order of flats:   B  E  A  D  G  C  F      (the exact reverse — fifths downward)

You don't memorize two lists; you memorize one direction of the circle. The mnemonic "Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle" (sharps) read backward is "Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father" (flats) — because flats are just fifths in the other direction.

There's even a shortcut baked in: the last sharp in a key signature is the leading tone (one semitone below the tonic), and the second-to-last flat names the key. Both facts are just "read the circle."

3. The full index​

SharpsMajorMinorFlatsMajorMinor
0CAm0CAm
1GEm1FDm
2DBm2B♭Gm
3AF♯m3E♭Cm
4EC♯m4A♭Fm
5BG♯m5D♭B♭m
6F♯D♯m6G♭E♭m

Read the "sharps" side top-to-bottom and you're walking clockwise; the "flats" side is counterclockwise. They meet at the bottom.

4. The seam: where pitch is a circle but notation isn't​

Look at the meeting point. 6 sharps = F# and 6 flats = Gb are the same pitch in equal temperament — the same key on a piano — but different spellings. This is the enharmonic seam.

It exists because of the split we found in file 01:

  • Pitch is genuinely modular (Z/12Z) — F# and Gb are one point.
  • Notation is not modular — it's a linear stack of fifths (...Fb Cb Gb Db Ab Eb Bb F C G D A E B F# C# G#...) that never wraps. On paper you can keep sharpening forever (F##, B#, E#) and never reach Gb.

So the "circle" is really the spiral of file 01 with its two ends pinned together by equal temperament. The pin leaves a visible seam: near the bottom of the wheel, keys get double-labeled (F#/Gb, C#/Db, B/Cb) and beyond 7 accidentals you'd need double-sharps/flats no one wants to read. That's why in practice nobody writes in C# major (7 sharps) when Db major (5 flats) spells the identical pitches more cheaply.

The seam is not a flaw in the theory — it's the fingerprint of the helix we collapsed.

5. What to carry forward​

  • The circle is a minimal-edit sort of the twelve key signatures: neighbors differ by one accidental / share six of seven notes.
  • The order of sharps and flats is literally the circle read in each direction — one fact, not three.
  • The enharmonic seam at the bottom is where modular pitch and non-modular notation disagree — the leftover of the spiral.
