Reading the Map

You now know the circle is a minimal-edit sort of key signatures (file 02), closed by equal temperament (file 01). This file is about fluent reading: what the geometry of the wheel means, so that position, distance, and direction become information you can query at a glance.

The payoff idea, stated up front:

A major scale is seven adjacent slots on the circle — a contiguous arc — and the notes in that arc are already sorted into that key's chords.

1. The two rings: relative major and minor​

The standard wheel has two concentric rings. The outer ring is the major key; the inner ring is its relative minor, sitting on the same spoke because they share a key signature (same seven notes, different tonic).

outer:   C        inner:  Am        ← 0 accidentals, same seven pitches
outer:   G        inner:  Em        ← 1 sharp
outer:   F        inner:  Dm        ← 1 flat

Relative minor is a minor third below the major (C → A). Don't confuse it with the parallel minor (C major → C minor), which is not a neighbor — C minor lives three steps counterclockwise (it shares its signature with Eb major). That gap between "relative" (adjacent) and "parallel" (three steps away) is itself a useful fact, and we'll cash it in when we discuss modal interchange in file 07.

2. Distance = relatedness (shared tones)​

Because each step changes one note, circle distance is a direct proxy for how many notes two keys share:

Steps apartShared pitch classesFeel
07 / 7same key
16 / 7very close (I ↔ V, I ↔ IV)
25 / 7close
34 / 7getting foreign
61 / 7maximally distant — tritone away

The note directly opposite any key is a tritone away (six steps, six semitones — the axis of symmetry of the whole wheel). It shares the fewest notes and sounds the most "unrelated." Hold onto this antipode: it reappears as the tritone substitution (file 07) and as the most dramatic modulation (file 06).

3. The scale is an arc — and the arc is pre-sorted into chords​

Take the seven-slot window centered near C:

   F    C    G    D    A    E    B
  (IV) (I)  (V)  (ii) (vi) (iii)(vii°)

Every note of the C major scale is one of these seven consecutive slots — the scale is a contiguous arc, and everything outside the arc is a note outside the key. But there's a bonus most posters never point out: reading the arc left-to-right sorts C major's diatonic chords by function:

  • F C G — the three primary triads IV I V, clustered dead-center. These are the "one accidental away" neighbors, the harmonic core of the key.
  • D A E — their relative minors ii vi iii, the softer colors, sitting on the inner ring under those same slots.
  • B — vii°, the diminished chord, marooned at the bright edge of the arc.

So the circle isn't just a map of keys; within any single key it's already a map of that key's chords, ordered from most central (I) outward to most tense (vii°). This is why the next file — functional harmony — is essentially just "which direction on the arc pulls where."

4. Direction has a color: the brightness gradient​

Moving clockwise (sharpward) repeatedly raises a note each step; moving counterclockwise (flatward) lowers one. That gives the wheel a brightness axis:

  • Sharp side = brighter, more lifted (the extreme is Lydian, with its raised 4th).
  • Flat side = darker, heavier (the extreme is Locrian / Phrygian, with lowered 2nds and 5ths).

This is not mysticism — it's the same one-note-per-step fact viewed as timbre instead of key signature. You can even lay the seven modes on a straight line by this gradient (Lydian → Ionian → Mixolydian → Dorian → Aeolian → Phrygian → Locrian, each one flat darker). We build exactly that "chart" as a worked example in file 09 — it's the cleanest demonstration that a good chart is just the circle projected onto the one axis your question cares about.

5. A reading checklist​

When you look at any point on the wheel, you should be able to read off, instantly:

  1. Its key signature — count steps from C (file 02).
  2. Its five closest relatives — the two neighbors, their two relative minors/majors, and its own relative — i.e. the immediate cluster (file 06 formalizes this).
  3. Its dominant and subdominant — one step clockwise and one step counterclockwise (file 04).
  4. Its most distant key — straight across (the tritone).
  5. Its brightness — how far to the sharp or flat side it sits.

If those five reads are automatic, the rest of the series is application.
