Functional Harmony as Geometry

Functional harmony is usually taught as a table to memorize: "tonic is home, dominant wants to resolve, subdominant is in between." The circle turns that table into geometry — directions and distances you can see — and, better, explains why the pulls point where they do.

Root motion down a perfect fifth is the strongest resolution in tonal music, and the circle is nothing but a chain of those pulls. Read counterclockwise, every arrow points home.

1. The three functions are three positions​

Center the wheel on your tonic. The three primary functions are its immediate neighborhood:

   IV  ———  I  ———  V
 (subdom.) (tonic) (dominant)
 counterclockwise      clockwise
  • Tonic (I) — home. Zero tension, the reference point.
  • Dominant (V) — one step clockwise. Maximum tension toward home; contains the leading tone that wants to resolve up to the tonic.
  • Subdominant (IV) — one step counterclockwise. A gentler tension that typically sets up the dominant (it's the "away" that leads to the "further away that snaps back").

The everyday cadence I → IV → V → I is therefore a tiny oscillation around the tonic — one step flatward, back through home, one step sharpward — followed by the big move: the dominant falling a fifth back to I.

2. Why down a fifth resolves so hard​

Two reinforcing reasons, both visible once you look:

(a) The overtone argument. The tonic is the strongest overtone of the note a fifth below it isn't — rather, the dominant's root generates the tonic-ish partials weakly, but the decisive point is inversion: a root moving down a fifth is the single most "grounded" bass motion because the arrival note sits as a low, stable reference the departure note pointed at. Stacked down-a-fifth motions feel like repeatedly "landing."

(b) The tritone argument. The dominant seventh chord V7 contains a tritone between its 3rd (the key's leading tone) and its 7th (the key's 4th degree). That tritone is maximally unstable and has exactly one comfortable resolution: the leading tone rises a semitone to the tonic, and the 4th falls a semitone to the 3rd. Both voices collapse inward onto tonic-chord tones. The dominant resolves down a fifth because that's the motion that lets its tritone untie itself.

In C major, V7 = G B D F
        leading tone  B → C   (up a semitone)
        4th degree    F → E   (down a semitone)
   → both land on the C-major triad. That double resolution is "the pull."

3. Harmonic gravity: the whole circle leans counterclockwise​

Because down-a-fifth = counterclockwise, tonal music has a built-in gravity toward the flat side. Chains of dominants naturally spiral counterclockwise until they reach the tonic — which is exactly the "circle progression" of the next file. Sharpward motion (up a fifth) feels like lifting / setting up tension; flatward motion (down a fifth) feels like releasing / arriving.

You can summarize the entire vocabulary of root motion as three directions on the wheel:

MotionCircle directionCharacter
Down a fifth (V→I)one step counterclockwisestrongest resolution, "landing"
Up a fifth (I→V)one step clockwisebuilding tension, "lifting"
Down a third (I→vi, I→IV via relative)short hop / inner ringsmooth, deceptive, coloristic
Up a step (IV→V)a longer jump across slotsenergetic, non-resolving push

4. The predominant region, seen as an arc​

Modern function-theory groups chords into three families — Tonic, Predominant, Dominant — and on the arc from file 03 they fall into contiguous zones:

   F     C     G     D     A     E     B
  IV     I     V    ii    vi   iii   vii°
  └─PD─┘ └T┘  └──D──┘ └──PD/T──┘   └D┘
  • IV and ii are predominant (they set up the dominant); note ii is just the relative minor sitting under IV+one, sharing its subdominant flavor.
  • I, vi, iii cluster as tonic-ish (stable, home-adjacent colors).
  • V and vii° are dominant (they carry the tension-tritone).

So the standard functional flow PD → D → T (ii → V → I) is, geometrically, a walk from the left of the arc rightward into the dominant, then a fifth-drop home — which is the single most common progression in Western music, and the subject of the next file.

5. What to carry forward​

  • The three functions are three positions relative to your tonic: subdominant one step flatward, dominant one step sharpward, tonic in the middle.
  • Down-a-fifth resolves hardest because it's the motion that resolves the dominant's tritone — geometry backed by acoustics.
  • Tonal music has counterclockwise gravity: tension builds sharpward, releases flatward. Progressions (next file) are just structured walks along that gradient.
