Beyond the Basics

The "advanced" harmonic devices — secondary dominants, extended dominant chains, tritone substitution, negative harmony, modal interchange — sound like separate tricks. They aren't. Each is a small, named displacement on the circle you already know. This file is a tour of those displacements.

1. Secondary dominants: borrow a neighbor's dominant​

A secondary dominant V/x ("five of x") is the dominant chord of some scale degree other than the tonic. Geometrically it's a one-chord tonicization (file 06): step to the slot one position clockwise from x and grab its dominant, so that x momentarily feels like a local tonic.

In C major, to tonicize Dm (ii):
  the dominant of D is A7  →  so  V/ii = A7
  A sits one step clockwise of D on the circle.
  Progression:  C  →  A7  →  Dm   ( I → V/ii → ii )

Chaining these walks you clockwise around the wheel, each V/-chord pulling counterclockwise to its target — tension out, resolution in, one slot at a time.

2. Extended dominant chains: the circle progression, chromaticized​

Make every chord in a fifth-drop chain a dominant seventh and you get an extended dominant sequence — pure counterclockwise motion where each chord is the secondary dominant of the next:

E7 → A7 → D7 → G7 → C
(III7)(VI7)(II7)(V7)(I)

This is just the circle progression of file 05 with every link "supercharged" into a dominant. It's the engine of ragtime turnarounds, gypsy jazz, and the bridge of "rhythm changes." On the wheel: a straight counterclockwise slide, every step pulling home.

3. Tritone substitution: swap for the antipode​

Recall (file 04) that V7 resolves because of the tritone between its 3rd and 7th. Here's the key symmetry: that same tritone belongs to exactly one other dominant seventh — the one whose root is a tritone away (the antipode on the circle).

G7  = G B D F        tritone = B–F
Db7 = Db F Ab Cb(B)  tritone = F–Cb(=B)   ← same two notes, B and F

Both chords contain the B–F tritone, so both resolve to C — and you can substitute Db7 for G7. Geometrically you've jumped to the opposite point of the circle (six steps), but harmonically nothing breaks because the resolving tritone is invariant. The payoff is a smooth chromatic bass descent Db → C instead of the leap G → C, which is why ii–bII7–I is everywhere in jazz. Antipodes on the circle share a tritone; that shared tritone is the whole trick.

4. Negative harmony: reflect across an axis​

Negative harmony is a reflection symmetry of the circle. Pick the axis running between the tonic and its dominant (in C, the axis through the midpoint of C and G) and mirror every note across it. The reflection:

  • swaps major and minor quality,
  • turns dominant function into subdominant function (clockwise pulls become counterclockwise ones),
  • maps a progression to its "shadow" that resolves with the same strength from the opposite side.
Mirror of  G7 (dominant of C)  across the C–G axis  →  a subdominant-minor
chord (Dm6 / Fm-ish) that also pulls to C, but from the flat side.

You don't have to buy the mystique around negative harmony to use the geometry: it's simply "what does my progression look like reflected across the tonic axis?" — a systematic reharm generator built from one circle symmetry.

5. Modal interchange: reach into the parallel window​

Borrowed chords (modal interchange) pull chords from the parallel key — which, from file 03, is not a neighbor but sits three steps flatward (C major ↔ C minor, whose signature matches Eb major). Borrowing iv, bVI, bVII, or bII from the parallel minor reaches into that flat-side window for darker color while keeping the same tonic.

In C major, borrow from C minor:
  iv  = Fm     bVI = Ab     bVII = Bb     bII = Db (the Neapolitan)

Each borrowed chord is a controlled reach toward the flat side of the wheel — the "darkening" direction from file 03 — without actually modulating. It's the coloristic counterpart to the sharpward reach of secondary dominants.

6. The unifying picture​

DeviceCircle move
Secondary dominantstep one slot clockwise of the target, borrow its V
Extended dominant chainstraight counterclockwise slide, every link a V7
Tritone substitutionjump to the antipode (shared tritone survives)
Negative harmonyreflect across the tonic–dominant axis
Modal interchangereach flatward into the parallel-key window

Five "advanced topics," five elementary operations on one wheel: step, slide, reflect, jump to the antipode, reach across. That's the whole point of the circle — it makes the exotic look like small motions.
