Modulation as a Sliding Window

File 03 showed that a key is a seven-slot arc on the circle. Modulation — changing key — is therefore the most literal thing imaginable:

Modulation is sliding the seven-slot window along the circle. How far you slide is how far you modulate; the notes in the overlap are your bridge across.

This single picture unifies "closely related keys," "pivot chords," and "modulation distance" into one geometric fact.

1. Closely related keys = adjacent windows​

Slide the C-major window by one step and you get G major (or, flatward, F major). The two windows overlap in six of seven notes — they're nearly the same scale. Formally, the five closely related keys of any major key are:

For C major:
  G  major   (I → V,  window slid +1 sharpward)
  F  major   (I → IV, window slid +1 flatward)
  Am minor   (relative minor, same window)
  Em minor   (relative of G)
  Dm minor   (relative of F)

These are exactly the keys within one step on the circle (counting both rings). They're "close" for a concrete, countable reason: their scales differ from the home scale by at most one accidental, so you can move between them almost without introducing foreign notes.

2. Pivot chords = the overlap region​

The smooth way to modulate is the pivot (common) chord: a chord that is diatonic to both the old window and the new one. Geometrically, it lives in the overlap of the two seven-slot arcs.

C major window:  F  C  G  D  A  E  B
G major window:      C  G  D  A  E  B  F#
overlap:             C  G  D  A  E  B        ← six shared notes

The chords built on those shared notes (in C→G: C=I/IV, Em=iii/vi, G=V/I, Am=vi/ii, etc.) can each be reinterpreted — heard as belonging to the old key on the way in and the new key on the way out. More window overlap = more pivot options = smoother modulation. Adjacent keys overlap in six notes (many pivots); distant keys overlap in one or two (few or no pivots, so you need a more abrupt technique).

3. Distance is a metric — and it has direction​

Because window overlap decreases predictably with circle distance, number of circle steps is a usable metric for "how big" a modulation feels:

Steps slidNew accidentalsModulation feel
11barely a modulation (tonicization-adjacent)
22clear but comfortable
3–43–4a real journey; few common chords
66the tritone jump — maximal, dramatic, no diatonic pivot

And the slide has the brightness direction from file 03:

  • Sharpward (clockwise) modulations lift — the classic "gear-shift up a whole step" in pop is really two clockwise steps, and it feels like a lift.
  • Flatward (counterclockwise) modulations darken / relax — moving toward the subdominant side.

4. When windows barely overlap: the abrupt techniques​

For distant modulations there's no common diatonic chord, so you borrow other bridges:

  • Common-tone modulation — pivot on a single shared note (not a whole chord), reharmonizing it into the far key. Works even for the tritone jump, since any two keys share at least one pitch.
  • Chromatic / direct modulation — just go, letting a strong dominant of the new key assert itself (often V7 of the target, which points home by the fifth-drop of file 04).
  • Enharmonic modulation — exploit the seam (file 02): a chord spelled one way in the old key is respelled to belong to a far key. The diminished-seventh and augmented-sixth chords are favorites because they're symmetric and can be reinterpreted into many windows at once.

5. Modulation vs. tonicization (a boundary worth drawing)​

Sliding the window and staying there is modulation. Briefly leaning one step over and snapping back is tonicization — you tip toward a neighbor for a chord or two without committing. The mechanism is the same (borrow the target's dominant), only the duration differs. Tonicization is the doorway to secondary dominants, which is exactly where the next file begins.

6. What to carry forward​

  • Modulation = sliding the seven-slot window; overlap = shared notes = ease.
  • Pivot chords live in the window overlap; more overlap (closer keys) = more pivots = smoother.
  • Circle steps are a distance metric with a brightness direction; distant jumps drop diatonic pivots and force common-tone / chromatic / enharmonic bridges.
