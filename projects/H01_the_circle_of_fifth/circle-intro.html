⭕ The Circle of Fifths

Most people meet the circle of fifths as a poster on a music-classroom wall: a pretty wheel you're told to memorize. That framing is a trap. It presents a result as if it were an axiom.

This series takes the opposite stance. The circle of fifths is not a rule — it's a data structure. Specifically, it's a lossy projection of a higher-dimensional object (the space of 12 pitch classes and their relationships) down onto a single ring, chosen because that particular projection makes one class of questions cheap to answer:

"Given where I am, what is harmonically nearby, and which direction pulls home?"

Everything else in this series follows from taking that sentence seriously.

How this series is organized​

The files are ordered as foundation → application → meta, and you can read the table as a dependency graph rather than a syllabus.

  • Foundation (01–03) — what the circle actually is. Where it comes from (stacking fifths until the tuning system forces the ring shut), why it doubles as the sorted index of every key signature, and how to read the map.
  • Harmony (04–07) — what it's for. Functional harmony as geometry, progressions as walks around the ring, modulation as a sliding window, and the "advanced" moves (secondary dominants, tritone sub, negative harmony) as small named displacements on the same circle.
  • Meta (08–09) — the honest part. The circle is one projection among many (Tonnetz, chromatic circle, Coltrane circle). This section is about knowing which chart answers which query — and how to build the minimal chart for a question you keep asking, instead of collecting other people's diagrams.

The one idea to hold onto​

Every chart in music theory trades away some relationships to make others visible. There is no "master chart," only projections tuned to queries. If you finish this series with a nose for which relationship a given diagram makes cheap to read, you're done — the rest is lookup.
