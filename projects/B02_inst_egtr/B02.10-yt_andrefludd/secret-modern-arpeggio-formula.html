The Secret Modern Arpeggio Formula

🎥 Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ht1x13a7gBA

Video: Andre Fludd — The Secret Modern Arpeggio Formula · 10:47 Subject: Tim Miller's 212 arpeggios, played on a Tim Miller signature guitar borrowed from a subscriber. Chapters: 0:00 concept · 1:01 the pattern · 2:02 the picking · 4:00 note selection · 6:29 extra tip · 7:40 third example · 9:16 extra tip 2 Key centres used: C major for the major-mode examples, A minor for the minor-mode ones.

The video's own tab is on screen and in his course; this page does not reproduce it. What's analysed here is the system — which is the part that transfers.

The idea in one sentence​

Fix a mechanical template (two notes, one note, two notes… across the strings), fix the picking that goes with it, and then spend all your attention on the one thing that actually decides how it sounds: which notes you put into it — chosen from a mode, and aimed at the note that makes that mode sound like itself.

Summary of the ideas​

  1. Legato patterns get stale because they're built on scale steps. Three notes per string, played in order, means most consecutive notes are a tone or a semitone apart. It sounds like a scale because it is a scale.

  2. The 212 pattern breaks that. Play two notes on a string, one on the next, two on the next, one, two, one. Fewer notes spread over the same six strings means bigger average intervals — the line covers more range and stops sounding like a run.

  3. The thing is deliberately simple. Fludd's framing: masters usually end up doing simple things that sound sophisticated. The pattern is countable on one hand; the sophistication is elsewhere.

  4. Split it into three components — the pattern, the picking hand, the note selection — and treat them separately. He's explicit that the third is the most important, and it's the one most players skip.

  5. The picking never changes. Tim Miller's version uses hybrid picking (pick + middle finger) with hammer-ons going up and pick strokes with pull-offs coming down. Fludd keeps the descending half and simplifies the ascending half to plain pick strokes plus hammer-ons. Either way you learn one right-hand move and it stays valid for every pattern you ever build.

  6. Note selection is Holdsworth-flavoured. Instead of "an arpeggio is the notes of a chord", use: an arpeggio is any group of notes from the mode you want to hear. He admits this is not what the word arpeggio means — it's a working definition, not a claim about theory.

  7. Then aim at the characteristic note. Every mode has one or two notes that are the reason it's not just "major" or "minor". Build the 212 shape so those notes land in it. A plain C major 7 arpeggio has nothing in it that says this mode and not another one, which is exactly why it sounds flat.

  8. Two extra tricks that make it sound un-guitar-like: run the shape along the opposite diagonal to the one guitar shapes normally lean along, and allow notes that sit directly above each other — same fret, adjacent strings — instead of always stepping diagonally.

Deep dive​

1. The problem being solved (0:00)​

The setup is a complaint, not a theory: legato patterns are great and they get boring. Worth being precise about why, because the fix follows from the cause.

A standard three-notes-per-string legato line plays scale degrees in order. On six strings that's eighteen notes, almost all of them a tone or semitone from their neighbour. The ear hears the second and third note and predicts the rest — which is the actual definition of boring.

So there are only two ways out: change the order of the notes, or change how many of them you play per string. The 212 pattern does the second, and the note selection section later does the first. That's the whole video, structurally.

2. Component one — the pattern (1:01)​

Counting from the low E string:

StringNotes played
6 (low E)2
5 (A)1
4 (D)2
3 (G)1
2 (B)2
1 (high E)1

Nine notes across six strings, against eighteen for a 3nps run. The repeating cell is really just 2-1; "212" is what you see when you look at any three adjacent strings at once.

Why fewer notes per string means wider intervals. Adjacent guitar strings are a fourth apart. When you play three scale notes on a string you use up roughly a third of an octave before crossing, so the crossing interval stays small. Take two of those notes away and the same six strings still have to cover the same range — so what's left between consecutive notes is bigger. The width isn't a mystical property of the number 212; it's arithmetic, and it's why the note selection step in part three has room to work at all.

He also notes you can start the cell from the A string instead. Same idea, and the reason to bother is that the pattern is position-independent: it's a rule about crossings, not a shape, so it moves anywhere.

3. Component two — the picking hand (2:02)​

Miller's ascending version, as described: pick the first note on the low E, hammer-on the second; pick the single note on the A; on the D string the first note is plucked with the middle finger and the second is hammered; pick the single note on the G; pluck-and-hammer again on the B; pluck the last note on the high E.

The logic underneath it: the finger takes over exactly on the strings where the pick would have to make an awkward crossing after a single note. The pick handles the strings it can reach comfortably; the middle finger handles the rest. Nothing is alternate-picked, so the line keeps a legato surface even though it's being attacked.

Descending is pick strokes and pull-offs together, with a sweeping motion.

Fludd's own version: he keeps Miller's descending pattern, which he likes, and drops the hybrid picking on the way up in favour of plain pick strokes with hammer-ons. He's explicit that this is preference, not correction — if you like Miller's, use it.

The load-bearing sentence in this whole section comes later, at 9:29: the picking always stays the same, no matter which notes you choose. That makes the right hand a fixed cost. You pay it once, and every pattern you invent afterwards is free mechanically. It's the same economics as The Practice Loop: isolate one variable, and make sure the variable you're isolating is the one that carries the music.

4. Component three — note selection (4:00)​

This is the part he calls most important, and he's right.

The reframe​

The ordinary definition — an arpeggio is the notes of a chord — has a consequence nobody notices: it decides your notes for you. A C major 7 arpeggio is C, E, G, B, and there is no choice left to make. Play it in a 212 shape and you get a slightly unusual delivery of an entirely ordinary sound.

The reframe, credited to how Allan Holdsworth thought about it: take the seven notes of the mode, and treat any subset of them as fair game. The pattern supplies the wide intervals; the mode supplies the legal notes; you supply the choice. He notes plainly that the result isn't an arpeggio in the strict sense. Fine — the word is doing a job here, not making a claim.

Then aim at something​

Free choice on its own doesn't help; seven notes chosen at random still sound like the scale. His second move is the one that turns it into a method:

look for the note that makes that mode special, and highlight it.

Mode by mode, as covered in the video:

ModeQualityCharacteristic noteWhy it's the target
Ionian (major)majorperfect 4ththe only degree separating it from Lydian; the 3rd and 7th just say "major"

Two modes are missing from the video — Aeolian (♭6 as its identifier against Dorian) and Locrian (♭5). The method transfers unchanged; he says as much: pick any mode, find what's special, build a 212 around it.

The worked example is the first one. A plain C major 7 arpeggio in a 212 shape is "fine but boring", because nothing in C-E-G-B identifies a mode. So he swaps in the perfect fourth where the third would normally be, and ends the line on the 9th. Two substitutions, and the same pattern stops sounding generic.

The perfect-fourth question​

Worth pausing on, because it sits oddly next to the advice most books give, and the two fit together once you see how.

Over a Cmaj7 chord, the 4th (F) sits a semitone above the 3rd (E). Sustain it and you get a harsh minor 9th interval against a chord tone — which is why the 4th is the classic avoid note over major 7 chords, and why so much teaching material tells you to leave it out or sharpen it.

Both things are true at once, and the resolution is about duration and role:

  • As a sustained note over a static Cmaj7, the natural 4th does clash. The standard fixes are to keep the 3rd out of the way, or resolve the 4th down to the 3rd, or play Lydian and sharpen it instead.
  • As a passing note inside a fast legato line, it is the strongest available signal that you're in Ionian and not Lydian — precisely because it's the only degree that distinguishes them.

His usage is the second one. The idea generalises well past this video: the characteristic note is often the uncomfortable one, and you make it work by controlling how long it rings rather than by avoiding it.

5. Extra tip — reverse the diagonal (6:29)​

Standard guitar arpeggio shapes — major, minor, diminished, from any starting string — all lean the same way across the neck. They were learned from the same sweep-picking vocabulary, so they share a geometry, and a listener who has heard a lot of guitar has heard that geometry many times.

His Lydian pattern deliberately slants the opposite way. Same notes available, same mode, but the line moves across the neck against the habitual grain, which forces interval choices the usual shapes never produce. On the final note, where the reversed slant makes the stretch awkward, he offers two outs: barre it, or slide into it.

The general principle, and the reason this tip is better than it first sounds: the shapes you know constrain the notes you choose, silently. Change the geometry and you change the melody, without knowing any new theory. That's the same argument as shapes vs names — shapes are fast and they hide things.

6. Extra tip — stacked notes (9:16)​

Inside the Dorian example he points out a second habit-breaker: playing notes that sit directly above each other — the same fret on adjacent strings — instead of the diagonal step guitarists default to.

Two reasons this is worth stealing. Musically, same fret on adjacent strings is a fourth (a major third between G and B), so a stacked pair gives you a wide interval "for free" without a stretch. Mechanically, it needs a barre or a rolled finger, which is a different fretting-hand skill from everything else in the pattern — the one genuinely new physical thing in the video.

7. Third example and the close (7:40, 10:26)​

The Mixolydian example (his "example 3") does the same job for the ♭7 and, he notes, leans hard on the reversed diagonal again. Dorian and Phrygian follow quickly, deliberately, because by then the method is the point rather than the lick.

His closing summary states the payoff plainly: a wide intervallic sound and a legato sound, cheaply, provided you already know your modes and can play basic legato.

What to bring to it from elsewhere​

The video teaches one device, and a device needs a context around it. These are the pieces to have in place so it turns into music rather than another exercise.

  1. Rhythm and phrasing. The examples are nine-note runs, played straight, to show the shape clearly. Where a line starts, stops and breathes is its own skill, and it's what keeps any repeatable pattern sounding fresh.
  2. Something to play it over. The examples are modal — one sound, nothing moving underneath. Fitting the same notes to a progression is the subject of Playing Over Changes and Guide Tones.
  3. The modes themselves. The one real prerequisite is being able to find a mode's characteristic note anywhere on the neck. If that isn't there yet, it's the thing to build first — otherwise you end up practising the pattern, which is the easy half.
  4. The fingerings, from the video. Two of the ideas — the reversed slant and the stacked notes — are physical, and they're much clearer watched than read.

Building your own, without copying the licks​

The method is portable enough to use immediately, and the video explicitly invites it — pick any mode, any 212 arrangement you enjoy, and the picking still works.

  1. Pick a mode and write down its characteristic note (the table above).
  2. Write the cell out as degrees, not frets: two degrees, one degree, two degrees, … across the strings. Choose non-adjacent degrees on purpose — that's where the width comes from.
  3. Place the characteristic note somewhere it will be heard — the top of a cell, or the last note before a direction change — and don't let it ring over a chord tone it clashes with.
  4. Find it on the neck yourself and check it by ear, then keep the fingering that survives at slow tempo. Degrees on paper are not a fingering, and a shape that looks fine on paper can be unplayable.
  5. Don't change the right hand. That's the whole payoff of the system: one picking pattern, unlimited note content.

Practice​

Small, in the spirit of A Routine You Will Keep — this is a ten-minute item, not a session.

StageWhat you doDone when
1The bare 2-1-2 crossing pattern on scale notes, one mode, slow, no metronomethe crossings are silent and even without looking
2Same pattern with the picking fixed — Miller's or Fludd's, pick one and stop switchingyou can start it from either the low E or the A string without thinking
3Swap one note in for the characteristic note of the modeyou can hear the mode change when you swap the note in and out
4Rebuild it with the reversed slant, and once with a stacked pairthe barre/slide note doesn't buzz at tempo
5Play it over one chord, and try to end the line somewhere musicalit sounds like a phrase and not like an exercise

Record stages 3 and 5. The whole reason for the characteristic note is a sound, and sound is the only thing that tells you whether you got it — Progress Without a Teacher.

Where this sits in these notes​

  • The pattern-versus-content split is the same one running through this whole section: mechanics are cheap and finite, note choice is where the music is. See The Elements.
  • "Any note of the mode is available" is the grown-up version of Outside the Pentatonic — same move, larger note pool.
  • Characteristic notes are guide tones' cousins: a small number of notes carrying most of the identity. Compare Guide Tones, which does it for chords instead of modes.
  • The reversed diagonal is a direct illustration of the cost of shape-based thinking described in shapes vs names.

Where to slot it in: this is a Stage 3+ idea, since it assumes modes, position freedom and clean legato. If the fretboard isn't mapped yet, Stage 1 is the better use of the same ten minutes. The 212 concept is only four rules long and will keep.
