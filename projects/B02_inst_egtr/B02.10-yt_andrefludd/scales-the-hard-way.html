Did You Learn Guitar Scales The Hard Way?

🎥 Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_im1_lzYdbk

Video: Andre Fludd — Did You Learn Guitar Scales The Hard Way? · 16:13 · posted 21 Nov 2025 Subject: an argument that the three-notes-per-string system is a better way to learn the fretboard than CAGED Examples in: A major, with A minor pentatonic used once as an illustration No chapters on this one — timestamps below come from the transcript.

The title is the thesis: if you learned scales through CAGED, he thinks you took the longer road. What follows is nine or so reasons, delivered in order of increasing practicality.

The idea in one sentence​

Three notes per string matches the hand, the eye, and the picking hand all at once, so one set of seven shapes ends up carrying the scale, the modes, the chords, the arpeggios and the technique — instead of each of those being a separate thing to learn.

Summary of the ideas​

  1. The shapes are more uniform. Put the two systems side by side and the three-notes-per-string shapes are visibly more symmetrical. We're pattern-seeking creatures, so shapes that are easy to see are easy to memorise and eventually to internalise.

  2. The anchoring principle. Guitarists like to plant the first finger and build around it. His example is minor pentatonic box 1 with the index anchored at the fifth fret — which he suspects is exactly why so many classic licks in every genre live in that one spot. Every three-notes-per-string pattern works the same way.

  3. The hand naturally reaches about five frets, and five frets gives exactly three scale notes per string. So the pattern isn't an arbitrary box, it's the territory your hand can cover from one position. His line: you don't need to break out of boxes if you were never in one.

  4. The seven patterns are a step apart. Seven notes in the scale, seven patterns, and each one sits a half step or whole step from the next — so connecting them is a small slide rather than a new shape.

  5. Modes come free. Pattern two played from its own lowest note is Dorian; the next is Phrygian; played while still thinking in the parent key, they're just more ways to play that key. You don't learn modes separately, because you already fingered them.

  6. Chords and arpeggios live in the same anchor. A barre chord at the fifth fret anchors the same finger in the same place as the matching scale pattern, and the arpeggio overlays on top of both. Move to the next degree and the minor barre chord, the Dorian pattern and the minor arpeggio all line up together.

  7. Chromatic passing tones are just the gaps. The notes between the scale notes are already inside your hand's reach; fill them all in and you have the chromatic scale to draw passing tones from.

  8. It suits every picking style. Three notes per string is one repeatable mechanic: legato repeats the same hammer-on/pull-off cell, economy picking lines up naturally, and alternate pickers can pick two and hammer the third to keep the pattern consistent.

  9. The shapes make musical material, not just runs. With three notes on each string it's easy to reorder them into groupings and sequences — fast, that's a shred lick; slow, with repeated notes and dynamics, the same shape gives a lyrical line.

  10. The real argument is unification. Scales, modes, chords, arpeggios, chromatic notes and technique all sitting inside one framework, rather than five systems to keep in your head at once.

He also pauses in the middle for a short aside about methods in general, which is the part of the video I'd keep longest — see reason six below.

Deep dive​

1. The shapes look more like each other (0:21)​

The first argument is visual and he makes it before touching the guitar. Lay out the CAGED scale shapes and the seven three-notes-per-string shapes side by side, and the second set is plainly more regular. Since we recognise and remember patterns far better than we remember lists, the more regular set should be cheaper to learn.

It's a soft argument, and he presents it as one. It's also probably true, and it matters most at the very beginning, when the cost of learning is highest and the motivation is thinnest.

2. The anchoring principle (1:24)​

This is the reason worth keeping even if you never adopt the system.

The observation: the hand is happiest when one finger stays put and the others move around it. Minor pentatonic box 1 with the index at the fifth fret is the clearest case — the anchor is so comfortable that navigating the box takes no thought at all.

Then a hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: that comfort is why so much of the guitar's classic vocabulary was invented in that one shape, across blues, rock and jazz alike. The ergonomics of the instrument shaped the music that came out of it.

Whether or not that's the whole story, it's a good way to think about learning material: a shape that lets you anchor gets explored, and a shape that doesn't gets avoided. It's also a fair defence of the site's own starting point in Box One — the reason to start there isn't that it's the best shape, it's that it's the one your hand will actually stay in long enough to find something.

3. Five frets, three notes (2:53)​

The claim: the hand naturally covers about five frets, and five frets gives you exactly three notes of a major scale on a string.

That's arithmetic, and it's worth doing once so you know it's true rather than plausible. Major-scale steps are 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1 semitones. Any three consecutive notes therefore span either 3 or 4 semitones:

StepsSemitones spannedFrets needed
2 + 245
2 + 134
1 + 234

So the widest case needs a five-fret window and the others fit inside it. The system isn't fitted to the guitar by coincidence; it's fitted to the size of a hand and the shape of the major scale, and those two happen to agree.

His conclusion — you never have to break out of a box, because you were never in one — is a good way to put it. Three notes per string is still a position with edges, of course; what it doesn't have is the feeling of being stuck, because the next pattern is a slide away rather than a different shape to learn.

4. The repeated note, and the reason behind it (3:57)​

A small aside that says a lot about how he's thinking. He points out that his pattern contains a duplicated pitch, and explains why he keeps it: he wants to know every note his hand can reach from that position, including the one that appears in two places, whether or not he'd ever play them back to back. He also teaches a version with a position shift instead, and thinks both are worth having.

The cause is the tuning. Five of the six string pairs are a fourth apart, but G to B is a major third — one semitone narrower. Any shape holding a single hand position across all six strings has to absorb that one-fret discrepancy somewhere: either the hand shifts, or the note groupings overlap and a pitch repeats. Why EADGBE covers where that irregularity comes from and what it buys in return.

The mindset underneath is the useful part: he's building a map of what's reachable, not a lick. A map is allowed to have a redundant entry. This is the same distinction the drills section on this site draws between knowing the territory and playing something.

5. Seven patterns, one step apart (5:01)​

Stated as a fact in the video, and it's worth seeing why it must be true. Each pattern starts on the next note of the scale, so the distance between consecutive patterns is simply the scale's own step sequence: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. Walk all seven and you've walked one octave up the neck.

That's a pleasant thing to know, because it means the seven shapes aren't seven facts. They're one fact — the scale — seen from seven starting points, and the gaps between them spell the scale out again.

6. A word about methods in general (6:08)​

He stops to say that CAGED works, that most things work, and that he knows a well-known player who swears by it. Then this, which I think is the most valuable thirty seconds in the video:

His point, paraphrased: the best players in the world aren't amazing because of their method — they're amazing because they're gifted. He doesn't count himself among them, so he went looking for the most logical and efficient way to learn instead.

Two things worth taking from that. First, it's the right way to read any "best method" claim, including this one: a great player's method is evidence about that player, not about the method. Second, choosing a system because it's efficient for you is a legitimate reason — probably the only honest one available to someone learning without a teacher.

7. Modes, included at no extra charge (6:52)​

The worked example, slowly: in A major, the first pattern is A major. The second pattern starts on B. If you keep thinking in A major, it's simply another fingering of A major. If you decide B is home, the same fingering spells B Dorian — 1, 2, ♭3 and onwards. The next one, from C♯, is Phrygian. So you never have to set aside a separate week to learn Lydian in five positions; you fingered it the first time round.

There's a second half to it that's worth pairing with this from the start.

Fingering a mode and hearing a mode are two skills, and this gives you the first one. The pattern hands you the geography at no extra cost. The sound comes from somewhere else — from what the music underneath treats as home. Play the second pattern over an A chord and you'll hear A major however firmly you think "B Dorian"; a scale is a set of notes, and a mode is that set plus a gravity that the bass and the chords supply.

He's clear about this himself — his instruction is to think from the new root. The point for practice is simply that the shapes and the ear need to be trained together, which is what The Tonal Centre is for. The patterns give you the addresses; the backing track tells you who lives there.

8. Chords and arpeggios in the same neighbourhood (9:42)​

The A barre chord at the fifth fret anchors the index finger exactly where the A major three-notes-per-string pattern anchors it. So the chord, the scale and the arpeggio can be seen as one object rather than three. Move up to B and the minor barre chord, the Dorian pattern and the B minor arpeggio stack the same way.

Seeing the chord and the scale as one object is the thing CAGED is best known for, and this reason shows that three notes per string supports it just as well. That's useful to know either way round: whichever system you learn, the chord shape is what tells you where the safe notes are.

A way to hold the two systems in mind: three notes per string is organised around the hand — reach, evenness, picking mechanics — while CAGED is organised around the harmony, keeping the chord in view at all times. Most self-teaching players are shorter on the second than the first, which is what Chord Tones on the Neck works on.

9. Chromatic passing tones as gap-filling (11:49)​

A chromatic passing tone is a note from outside the scale used to lead into one inside it — his example is the ♭3 sliding into the major 3rd, which is most of the blues vocabulary in one gesture.

The insight is that you don't need a new map for them. The chromatic notes are the gaps between the notes you already know, and they're already under your hand; fill every gap and the shape becomes the chromatic scale. So the practical question stops being where are these notes and becomes which gap, and how briefly — which is a musical question, and a much better one.

This is the same territory as Outside the Pentatonic, approached from the other direction: that page adds specific notes for specific reasons, this one hands you all of them and asks you to choose.

10. Why it suits speed (13:12)​

Consistency. Every string has the same number of notes, so the picking hand learns one cell and repeats it:

StyleHow it lines up
Legatoone hammer-on/pull-off cell per string, repeated
Economy pickingthe string change always falls in the same place
Alternate pickingpick two, hammer the third, and the pattern stays regular

One thing to add, because it's the flip side of the same coin: three notes per string maps naturally onto groups of three, so these shapes tend to come out as triplets and sextuplets. That's what makes them fast, and it's also why long 3nps runs can start to sound like scales. Playing them in fours, against the grain of the shape, is harder and much more musical — and it's the standard fix.

11. "But that's not music" (14:36)​

He raises the objection himself and answers it fairly: running a scale isn't musical, but three notes on a string is a convenient unit for making sequences, and the same reordered cell can be a shred lick at one tempo and a lyrical phrase at another, depending on note order, repetition and pick dynamics.

Worth reading next to the 212 arpeggio video, which picks up where this one stops — the two are a sequence, and they do different jobs:

Three notes per string212 arpeggios
What it's forlearning and covering the neckgenerating lines that don't sound like scales
Note orderscale steps, in orderdeliberately skipped, wide intervals
When it helpswhile you're still mapping the keyonce the map exists and everything sounds too even

Learn the neck with the even system; reach for the uneven one when evenness becomes the thing holding a line back.

What to pair it with​

  1. The pentatonic. It shows up here once, as the anchoring example, and it's the shape most players actually solo with — so connecting the two maps is its own piece of work. He links a separate video for exactly that at the end.
  2. Note names. Any shape system tells you where to put your fingers, not what you're playing. Closing that gap is the subject of shapes vs names.
  3. Whatever your hand actually does. He notes that the five-fret reach is hard at first, and that injuries or arthritis need workarounds. If that's your situation, a four-fret system is a perfectly good place to work from.
  4. Time. Seven patterns across the neck, properly connected, is a project of months. Worth knowing before you start so the pace doesn't feel like failure.

Practice​

If you want to test the claim rather than take it on faith, this is roughly the smallest experiment that would tell you something. Ten minutes, in the spirit of A Routine You Will Keep.

StageWhat you doDone when
1Learn one 3nps pattern in one key, anchored, slowlythe anchor finger genuinely stays put
2Learn the pattern a step above it, and slide between the twothe join doesn't interrupt the rhythm
3Find the barre chord that shares the anchor with each patternyou can see chord and scale as one shape
4Play the second pattern over a backing on its own root, then over the parent key's rootyou can hear the mode change even though the notes didn't
5Reorder one pattern into a group of four notes and play it in 16thsit stops sounding like an exercise

Stage 4 is the one that decides whether the "modes for free" claim is true for you. If you can't yet hear the difference, the shapes aren't wrong — you just haven't done the ear half yet, and that's Stage 2 rather than more patterns.

Where this sits in these notes​

  • The site's Stage 1 starts from the pentatonic rather than 3nps, and does the travelling work early in Going Horizontal precisely to avoid the box prison this video is arguing against. Different route, same destination.
  • The Whole Neck is the natural place to add a 3nps layer, once the five pentatonic shapes are joined up.
  • The Tonal Centre is the necessary companion to reason five — the shapes give you modes on paper, that page gives you modes in the ear.
  • The 212 arpeggio page is the sequel in practice: what to do once every scale shape is available and they all sound alike.

Where to slot it in: a Stage 1 topic with Stage 2 consequences. If the pentatonic isn't mapped yet, learning a second system at the same time tends to slow things down. Once it is mapped, the seven patterns make a sensible next project. The anchoring idea in reason two is worth taking straight away, whichever system you end up using.
