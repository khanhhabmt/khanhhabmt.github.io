EVERY Guitar Chord Explained By A Music Professor

🎥 Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ekehQzUyFWM

Video: Andre Fludd — EVERY Guitar Chord Explained By A Music Professor · 31:54 · posted 18 Jul 2025 Subject: one system that generates every chord, instead of a dictionary of shapes Chapters: 0:00 intro · 0:50 step 1 · 5:25 sus chords · 9:40 add and 6 chords · 13:25 nailing the changes · 15:26 7th chords · 19:40 special minor · 25:58 extended chords Key used throughout: C major (and A minor for the tonic-minor section)

This is a theory video, not a technique video — nothing here needs a fingering, so unlike the 212 arpeggio page, almost all of it survives being written down. Below the summary there are a few derivations worked out in full, and pointers to where each topic continues.

The idea in one sentence​

Every chord in common use is produced by one operation — play a note, skip the next, repeat — applied to a scale; so the thing to learn is the operation and the naming convention, not the chords.

Summary of the ideas​

  1. A chord is three or more notes sounding together with a harmonic function. The working definition for the video. Chord dictionaries with thousands of shapes, in his view, answer the wrong question.

  2. Chords come from scales, by stacking thirds. Take a scale, play a note, skip one, play, skip, play. From C in the C major scale: C-E-G. Do it starting from each of the seven degrees and you get the seven chords of the key.

  3. The qualities always come out in the same order: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished. True in every major key — which is all the "number system" is. "Play the 4 chord" means the triad on the fourth degree, which is always major; a ii-V-I is just those three.

  4. There are four triads, not hundreds: major (1 3 5), minor (♭3), diminished (♭3 ♭5), augmented (3 ♯5). Only three occur in the major scale; augmented comes from somewhere else, which he explains much later.

  5. Sus chords are the two non-triad three-note chords: sus4 and sus2 — the 3rd replaced by the 4th or the 2nd. They're unstable and want to resolve; his image is stepping outside for a package rather than flying across the country.

  6. Everything sorts into three categories: major, minor, tension. Diminished, augmented, sus4, sus2 and dominant chords are tension — they must go home to a major or minor chord. This is the organising idea of the whole video.

  7. Four-note chords are triads plus one: add9 (triad + 9), 6 chords (triad + 6), 6/9, and the 7th chords. The naming is literal, and the number naming (2 = 9, 4 = 11, 6 = 13) only depends on whether the note is written above the octave.

  8. There are six 7th-chord types, four of them inside the major scale: major 7, minor 7, dominant 7, minor 7♭5. Diminished 7 and minor-major 7 come from harmonic minor.

  9. The dominant 7 is what the V chord really is. The ♭7 is treated as present even when nobody plays it; adding it is what moves the V from "major" to "tension".

  10. A minor 7♭5 and a minor 6 are the same four notes. Which one it is depends on which note you treat as the root. That's the door into tonic minor — writing music where a minor chord is home rather than a waypoint.

  11. Harmonic minor exists to fix the V chord. In natural minor the five chord is minor, so i-v-i lacks drama. Raise one note to make it dominant and you have created harmonic minor — which then supplies the augmented triad, the diminished 7, and the minor-major 7 that were owed from earlier. Melodic minor raises one more note and gives the minor 6.

  12. Extended chords are the same operation, continued. Keep stacking: 9, 11, 13. Naming an extension implies everything under it — C9 contains the ♭7, Cadd9 doesn't. That single distinction is most of the confusion around extended chord names.

  13. Major 11 is avoided because it mixes categories. The natural 11 (the 4th) against the major 3rd is a clash — effectively a sus chord and a major chord at once. Raise it to ♯11 and the clash disappears, which is why maj7♯11 is everywhere and maj11 isn't.

  14. The payoff example: F major 7♯11 comes from the plain C major scale. Play, skip, repeat starting on F. A chord that sounds advanced, built from nothing but the scale you already knew — and usable as a substitute for the IV chord, or as an arpeggio when soloing.

  15. The practical tip in the middle (13:25) is the most useful minute in the video — see the deep dive below.

Deep dive​

1. The premise (0:00)​

The framing is aimed at the chord-dictionary book: thousands of diagrams, memorised one at a time, producing no understanding. His claim is that one generative rule replaces all of it.

One thing to know going in, since it shapes what follows: three or more notes with a harmonic function is a harmony definition, so power chords sit outside it — two notes, no third, no major or minor identity to categorise. They're a sound rather than a harmony, which is why they don't come up here. Everything in this video is about the note content of full chords.

2. The engine: play, skip, repeat (0:50)​

Take the C major scale and, from any note, play one, skip one, play one, skip one. From C: C-E-G. From D: D-F-A. Seven degrees, seven chords.

He says to memorise the quality pattern. Don't — derive it once. A triad is two stacked thirds, and a third is either 4 semitones (major) or 3 (minor), depending on where the scale's two half steps fall. The combinations:

Lower 3rdUpper 3rdResult
major (4)minor (3)major triad
minor (3)major (4)minor triad
minor (3)minor (3)diminished
major (4)major (4)augmented

Run it through C major and the famous pattern falls out on its own:

DegreeNotesThirdsQuality
1C E G4 + 3major
2D F A3 + 4minor
3E G B3 + 4minor
4F A C4 + 3major
5G B D4 + 3major
6A C E3 + 4minor
7B D F3 + 3diminished

And it answers, immediately, a question he defers for twenty minutes: why isn't the augmented triad in the major scale? Because augmented needs two major thirds in a row — 8 semitones of stacking — and the major scale never offers that. It isn't a mystery to be resolved later; it's a gap in the arithmetic.

This is also exactly the ground covered by Chords From the Key in these notes. If you've read that page, the video's first five minutes are revision, and you can start at 5:25.

3. Sus chords, and the home/away model (5:25)​

He builds the I-V-I first, because sus chords only make sense against it: 1 is home, 5 is a place you visit and want to leave. The travel analogy (home in New Jersey, in-laws in San Diego, and the strong desire to return) is doing real work — it makes tension concrete rather than a word.

Sus chords are then a much smaller departure: you step outside and come straight back. Two of them only — sus4 (the more intense) and sus2 — and both can resolve to a minor chord as easily as a major one, since neither contains a 3rd to argue with.

The category model arrives here, and it's the video's real backbone:

CategoryChordsBehaviour
Majormajor triad, 6, add9, maj7, maj9, maj7♯11stable, can be home
Minorminor triad, m6, m(add9), m7, m9, mMaj7stable, can be home
Tensiondiminished, augmented, sus2, sus4, dominant 7, m7♭5, dim7unstable, must resolve

What the three categories do and don't decide. They sort chords by stability — whether a chord can be home or needs to move. That's the first question, and it's the one this video answers. The second question is where a chord goes: IV, ii and vi are all stable by this test but behave differently in a progression, since vi can stand in for I, IV pulls toward V, and ii sets up V. That layer is functional harmony, and it's the subject of his follow-up video.

4. Add chords, 6 chords, and the numbers above 7 (9:40)​

Straightforward, and the naming rule is the only thing to hold onto: past the octave, degree 1 is also 8, so 2 becomes 9, 4 becomes 11, 6 becomes 13. An add9 is a triad plus that note; a 6 chord is a triad plus the 6th, and it isn't called "add13" because the note wasn't written above the octave.

Two details worth keeping:

  • C6 means C major 6. The "major" is dropped in writing because a 6 chord built on a minor triad gets spelled Cm6.
  • A minor 6 chord uses a major 6th, which looks wrong and isn't — it's minor because of its ♭3, and the natural 6 is exactly what makes it interesting. He flags this and defers it; the answer arrives at 25:14 when melodic minor turns up.

5. The most useful minute: the notes that always work (13:25)​

The practical core of the video, and the part most worth acting on.

The familiar advice is to learn your triads and inversions and use them to follow the chords. His observation is that a three-note triad on its own gives a thin, predictable line, while the players teaching it are audibly using more notes than that in their examples — so the question of which extra notes is left open.

His fix: some chords are permanently interchangeable. Over a C major chord you can play, at any time and without asking anyone, C major triad, C6, Cmaj9, C6/9. They're all "major category" and none of them will be wrong. So when you learn the C triad and its inversions, learn where the 6th and the 9th sit next to those shapes at the same time. Same shape-learning effort, and the extra two notes are what turns a triad outline into something that sounds like a player.

This is the identical idea to the note-selection section of the 212 arpeggio video — the shape supplies the skeleton, a small number of chosen colour notes supply the identity — and it is the same move as guide tones, one level up. If you take one thing from 32 minutes, take this.

6. Seventh chords (15:26)​

Keep stacking. C-E-G plus B gives Cmaj7; do it on all seven degrees and the key of C yields Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7, Bm7♭5.

On the dominant 7. He returns to the promise he made at 3:11: the V chord is "major or dominant". The point is that the ♭7 is understood to be there whether or not it's played — which is why a plain G triad in the key of C already behaves like tension. Adding the ♭7 doesn't create the tension; it makes it explicit.

On the double-flat 7. He points out that the ♭♭7 of a diminished 7 chord is the same pitch as a major 6th, and calls the naming needlessly confusing. As a sound, that's exactly right — your ear can't tell them apart. The spelling does have a practical job, though, and it's worth knowing: chords built in thirds use every other letter name, so in C that's C, E♭, G♭, B♭♭. Keeping the letters alternating is what makes the stacked-thirds structure visible on the page and lets you read where the chord resolves. Write that note as A and the sound is unchanged, but the chord stops looking like what it is.

7. The special minor, and where the leftovers came from (19:40)​

The pivot: m7♭5 and m6 are the same four notes.

Cm6 = C E♭ G A · Am7♭5 = A C E♭ G

Same pitch set, different root, different name, different category — tension if you call it Am7♭5, stable minor if you call it Cm6. He tells the story of a student who loved the m7♭5 sound but couldn't build songs on it, because a tension chord can't be home; renaming it as m6 solved the problem instantly. That's a good illustration of something worth generalising: a chord's category depends on what the music treats as home, not only on the notes.

Then the derivation of harmonic minor, which is the cleanest passage in the video. In natural minor the v chord is minor, so i-v-i has no pull. Change the third of that chord from minor to major and the v becomes a dominant 7 — and that one altered note, the raised 7th of the scale, is what defines harmonic minor.

Worth naming the note precisely: what's raised is scale degree ♭7 → ♮7, which creates a leading tone a semitone under the root. That semitone pull is the whole mechanism. The side effect is a gap of three semitones between ♭6 and ♮7, and that gap is the "dark, classical" sound he describes.

Harmonic minor then pays every debt in the video at once. In A harmonic minor:

DegreeChordSettles which promise
iAm(maj7)the sixth and last 7th-chord type
IIICaug (C E G♯)where the augmented triad lives
VE7the dominant that natural minor couldn't produce
viiG♯dim7where the fully diminished 7 lives

Melodic minor is then one further change — the ♭6 raised too — and its home chord is the minor 6, which retroactively explains why a minor 6 chord uses a natural 6th. Tonic-minor music can therefore use either mMaj7 or m6 as home.

One more seventh chord, if you want it. His six are the ones you'll meet constantly, and they're a good working set. There's a seventh hiding in the same material: the III of harmonic minor, taken all the way to four notes, is C-E-G♯-B — an augmented major 7. Past these three scales there are further types again (7♯5, 7♭5, the altered dominants), which is where jazz harmony picks up the thread.

8. Extended chords (25:58)​

Same operation, continued past the 7th, and one rule that matters:

Naming an extension implies everything beneath it. Cmaj9 is not a triad plus D — it also contains B, the major 7th. Dm9 contains the ♭7. Hence the distinction that trips up most players:

ChordNotes in CCount
Cadd9C E G D4
C9C E G B♭ D5

Follow the stack to its end and something tidy falls out: 1-3-5-7-9-11-13 is the entire seven-note scale, re-sorted into thirds. A 13th chord isn't an exotic object; it's the key itself, stacked. Which is also why the chord ladder stops there — the next skip lands back on the root.

Why maj11 is avoided. Adding the natural 11 (the 4th) to a major chord puts a semitone against the major 3rd, and mixes his own categories: a sus sound and a major sound at once. He's honest about the status of the rule — use it if you like it, play it enough and it starts to sound good, and if you make it work someone will eventually name it after the fact.

Then the payoff. Apply play-skip-repeat starting on F inside C major: F-A-C-E-G-B, which is Fmaj7♯11 — the ♯11 being B, already in the key. A chord that sounds like jazz education, built from nothing. On guitar you'll often drop the 9th to make it fit under the hand, which he mentions and which is the only voicing advice in the video.

Two things to add:

  • Fmaj7♯11 is the IV chord, and the IV chord is Lydian. The reason no accidental is needed is that the fourth degree of a major scale, treated as a root, is the Lydian mode — and ♯11 is Lydian's characteristic note. That is the same note, doing the same job, as in the 212 arpeggio video.
  • The 4th is the problem note in both videos. In the arpeggio video he deliberately targets the natural 4th over a major sound; here he says the natural 11 clashes and should be raised. Both are right, and the reconciliation is the same as before: a 4th that passes in a fast line is colour, a 4th that is held inside the chord is a clash. Duration decides.

What comes next​

This video answers "what notes are in the chord". Four further topics sit on top of it, each a separate piece of work:

  1. Voicings. Six strings and four fingers means every chord on guitar is a choice about which notes to play — what to drop (usually the 5th, often the root), what to double, where to put the 3rd and 7th. He touches it once, when dropping the 9th from Fmaj7♯11 to make it fit the hand.
  2. The chords that aren't built in thirds. Power chords, quartal voicings and open-string clusters follow different logic, and they're a large part of the electric guitar's sound.
  3. Where chords go. Categories tell you what a chord wants; progressions tell you where it goes. That's his follow-up video, and here it's functional harmony and progressions.
  4. Recognising them by ear. The skill that turns all of this from knowledge into something usable on the bandstand — Hearing the Changes.

Practice​

Theory that isn't converted into something your hands do evaporates in a week. Small enough to fit the routine:

StageWhat you doDone when
1Say the seven chords of a key out loud, from the quality pattern, in three different keysno hesitation, no counting on fingers
2Derive one triad's quality from its two thirds rather than recalling ityou can explain why vii is diminished
3Take one major triad shape you know and find the 6th and the 9th next to ityou can add either without moving position
4Play I-V-I, then I-sus4-I, then I-V7-I, and listen for the difference in pullyou can hear which one wants to resolve harder
5Build maj7♯11 on the IV chord of a key you use, and play it as a chord and as an arpeggioit sounds like a colour you chose, not a shape you found

Stage 3 is the one that pays back fastest — it's the video's own tip, and it's the bridge from chord knowledge to soloing.
