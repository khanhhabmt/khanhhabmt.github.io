I Stopped Memorizing Chords and Did This Instead

🎥 Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=15rCY_Bsb6U

Video: Andre Fludd — I Stopped Memorizing Chords and Did This Instead · 19:34 · posted 21 Aug 2026 Subject: one routine that teaches chord shapes, intervals, the fretboard and voicings at the same time Key used: C major, worked along the A string Designed for: five to ten minutes a day — he built it for a student with almost no practice time He mentions a free extended lesson and PDF with the fingerings and the theory he skips here; the links are in the video description.

The practical companion to the chord theory video. That one answers what notes are in the chord; this one is about where those notes sit under your hand and how to find them without memorising more shapes.

The idea in one sentence​

Walk the seven chords of a key up a single string, and at each one learn the intervals inside the shape — then every nearby chord becomes a small move of one finger rather than a new shape to memorise.

Summary of the ideas​

  1. The guitar's problem, stated fairly. A pianist plays C-E-G as three keys in a straight line and adds notes with spare fingers. A guitarist has four fingers and six strings tuned in a way that doesn't lay the notes out linearly, so shapes get memorised instead of understood.

  2. Where the routine came from. A student with a busy schedule wanted something she could do in five or ten minutes when she picked the guitar up. That constraint shaped everything: it has no end state, and any single session is worth doing.

  3. Play the scale along one string. Instead of a scale in one position, run C major up the A string. Now the scale is a line you can see.

  4. A scale carries three layers at once: the note names, the degree numbers, and a chord quality attached to each degree — major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished. That pattern is all the Nashville number system and Roman numerals are.

  5. Play the chord for each degree, up the string. That's a harmonized scale, and it puts all seven chords of the key in one line under one hand.

  6. You only need four shapes. Major and minor, each with the root on the A string and on the low E string. Diminished is the one extra, and it can wait.

  7. The actual work is learning the intervals inside the shape — which string holds the root, which the 3rd, which the 5th — plus the notes around them you could reach. That's the step he says separates this from ordinary chord practice.

  8. New chords are then one finger moving. Root down to the 7th gives a major 7. Releasing the finger on the 3rd lets the 2nd sound, which is a 9th. Adding the 6th on top gives a 13th. Same shape, same position, different chord.

  9. On guitar you're always free to subtract notes. A maj9 is 1-3-5-7-9 on paper; on guitar you play whichever of those fit the hand.

  10. Names matter less than category. As long as you know it's a major chord that also contains the 2nd and the 6th, the label is a convenience.

  11. Repeat the process on the minor shape, which is the major shape with one note moved, and you've covered nearly every chord in the key — six of the seven are major or minor.

Terminology​

TermWhat it means

Deep dive​

1. Why shapes pile up in the first place (0:00)​

The comparison with the piano is the clearest statement of the guitar's actual difficulty. On a keyboard, C-E-G is three keys in a straight line, and the relationship between the notes is visible in the layout. On a guitar the same three notes are scattered across strings tuned in fourths with one major third in the middle, so the visible thing is a shape, and the shape doesn't tell you what it contains.

Everything in this video follows from that: if the shape won't tell you what's inside it, you have to put the contents back in yourself, once, and then the shape becomes readable.

2. Playing the scale along one string (1:27)​

The first move is small and does more than it looks. Instead of a scale in one position, play C major up the A string.

Two things become visible that a positional shape hides:

  • The scale's step pattern turns into fret distances. Whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half becomes 2 frets, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1 — the shape of the major scale as a measurement rather than a fingering.
  • Every root for a barre chord is now on one line. Since the A string carries each degree in order, the chord for each degree is at the fret you just landed on.

This is the same move as Going Horizontal in these notes, which does it with the pentatonic and for the same reason — travelling along the neck before shapes harden into islands. Worth noticing that two different teachers arrive at single-string work early, for different material.

3. The three layers inside a scale (2:11)​

He points out that a scale hands you more than notes: names, numbers, and a chord quality per degree. The qualities in a major key are always major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished, and that fixed pattern is the whole of the number system.

If you want the derivation — why those qualities in that order — it's worked out in the notes on the chord theory video, where the two stacked thirds of each degree are counted out.

4. The harmonized scale up one string (3:15)​

Play the barre chord for each degree in turn, moving up the A string, and you have the key laid out as a line. He's relaxed about it in practice: on the shapes where the stretch is uncomfortable he drops the top note or adjusts, and says so.

He also mentions here that the video was getting long, so the full fingerings and the theory he passes over live in a free extended lesson and PDF linked from the description. If you're going to work through this, that's worth collecting first.

5. The step that makes it a system: interval mapping (5:05)​

This is the centre of the video.

Take one shape and name what each string is doing. For the major barre with its root on the A string:

StringWhat it plays
Aroot
D5th
Groot
Bmajor 3rd
high E5th

The minor version of the same shape differs by exactly one note: the major 3rd on the B string becomes a ♭3. Everything else is identical.

That's the whole memorisation load he asks for — this map, for major and minor, with the root on the A string and on the low E string. Four shapes, and one of them is the other with a finger moved.

6. New chords as one-finger moves (6:50, 13:27)​

Once you know which string holds which interval, you stop hunting for chords and start editing the one you're holding.

Starting shapeThe moveWhat you get
Majorroot on the G string down one fret → major 7thmajor 7
Majorrelease the finger on the B string, so the barre's note sounds — the 3rd becomes the 2ndmajor 9
Majoradd the 6th on the high E stringmajor 13
Majorkeep root, 5th and the 9tha power-chord-like voicing with a 9th
Minorroot on the G string down two frets → ♭7minor 7
Minorreach for the 4thminor 11
Minormove that 4th down to the ♭3, then on to the 9thfurther minor colours

Two details worth pulling out, because they're the arithmetic behind the moves.

How far the root moves to become the 7th depends on the chord. A major 7th is a semitone below the root, so the finger moves one fret. A ♭7 is a whole tone below, so it moves two. That single fact explains both the major and the minor version of the move, and it's why the same gesture gives you maj7 in one shape and m7 in the other.

Why releasing the finger on the B string produces a 9th. In this shape the major 3rd sits two frets above the barre. Let the barre's own note sound instead and you've dropped a whole tone, from the 3rd to the 2nd — and past the octave the 2nd is called the 9th. The chord loses its 3rd in the process, which is part of why it sounds open rather than sweet.

7. Subtracting notes (10:28)​

On paper a major 9 is 1-3-5-7-9. Under four fingers it usually isn't, so he plays root, 5th, 7th and 9th and moves on, with the rule stated plainly: you're always free to subtract notes.

The natural next question is which note to drop, and there's a conventional order worth knowing:

NoteHow droppableWhy
5thfirst to gosays almost nothing about the chord's identity
Rootoften droppablethe bass or the band usually supplies it
3rdkeep if you canit's what makes the chord major or minor
7thkeep if you canit's what makes it a 7th chord rather than a triad
The extension you namedkeepit's the reason you chose the chord

That's the same principle as Guide Tones applied to chords instead of lines: a small number of notes carry the identity, and the rest are optional.

8. Naming is secondary (11:37)​

A nice piece of pressure relief. If you can't remember whether to call it a major 13, it doesn't matter much, as long as you know it's a major chord that also has the 2nd and the 6th in it. The category tells you how the chord behaves; the name is a label for talking to other people.

That said, the second-identity rule is small enough to keep: past the octave, 2 becomes 9, 4 becomes 11, 6 becomes 13, because you carry on counting instead of starting again.

9. Running it through the whole key (16:20)​

With major and minor mapped, the same treatment applies to every degree: 1 major, 2 minor, 3 minor, 4 major, 5 dominant, 6 minor, 7 diminished. Six of the seven are handled by the two shapes you already mapped, which is why four shapes go so far.

Two he flags and sets aside for the extended lesson: the ♯11 available on the 4 chord, and the dominant on the 5, which he says is the one genuinely worth learning beyond major and minor. If you want the theory behind both before then, the ♯11 on the IV chord is worked through in the chord theory notes.

10. On scope (18:52)​

He closes by saying that one video can't be a whole method, and that yes, there are other things a player also has to do. It's a small remark and a sane one — worth carrying to every lesson video, this one included.

What to pair it with​

  1. The extended lesson and PDF he links. The fingerings are on screen only, and this page deliberately doesn't reproduce them.
  2. A backing chord or a loop. The routine is exploratory by design, so without something to play against, the "very pretty" moments are hard to judge. Even a single sustained bass note makes the differences obvious.
  3. The theory for the two chords he defers — the dominant on the 5 and the ♯11 on the 4.
  4. Ear work eventually. Knowing that lifting one finger produced a 9th is different from hearing a 9th and reaching for it — Hearing the Changes.

Practice​

The routine is already built for ten minutes, so this is really just his shape with the checkpoints made explicit. It fits the argument in A Routine You Will Keep better than almost anything else in this folder.

StageWhat you doDone when
1Play one major scale along the A string, saying the degree numbersthe fret distances feel like the scale, not like counting
2Play the barre chord on each degree, up and backyou know the quality of each degree without checking
3Name what each string plays in the major shape, then the minoryou can say "that's the 3rd" while holding the chord
4Take one chord and find maj7, 9, 13 by moving a single fingerthe moves come from the interval map, not from memory
5Do the same on the minor shape, then pick one new voicing to keepyou use it in something you actually play

Stage 5 is the one that turns it from exploration into repertoire. One kept voicing per week is a good rate.

  • Chord Tones on the Neck is the soloing version of the identical habit: know which note is which inside a shape you already hold.
  • The chord theory video supplies the naming and the reasons; the two pages are a matched pair.
  • The scales video explains why this works so well: the barre finger is an anchor, and everything here is explored around it without moving the hand.
  • Shapes vs names is the underlying issue — this routine is a direct answer to it, converting shapes you already play into names you can reason about.

Where to slot it in: any stage. It needs a few barre chords and nothing else, it costs ten minutes, and it pays into chord vocabulary, interval knowledge and fretboard mapping at the same time. Of the four videos in this folder, this is the one to start with if you only pick one.
