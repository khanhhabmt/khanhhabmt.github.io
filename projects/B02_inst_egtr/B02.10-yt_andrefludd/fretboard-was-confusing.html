The Fretboard Was Confusing Until I Did This

🎥 Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d6FMNRAnhbY

Video: Andre Fludd — The Fretboard Was Confusing Until I Did This · 15:05 · posted 14 Aug 2026 Subject: the order in which to learn things on the fretboard — shape first, analysis layered on afterwards Source he's drawing on: two passages from Guitar Zero by Gary Marcus, read in his community's book club

The odd one out in this folder. There's no lick to learn and no chord to build — it's about how to learn the other four, which makes it the page to read first if you're deciding where your practice time goes.

The idea in one sentence​

Learn the shape by rote until you can see it with your eyes closed and play it without thinking, and only then layer the intervals, note names and theory on top — because seeing and doing are handled by a different part of you than knowing, and they're faster.

Summary of the ideas​

  1. The fretboard gives you nothing to grip. A piano lays its structure out in white and black keys; a fretboard shows a grid with no visible organisation. So guitarists memorise: chord shapes, scale patterns, interval relationships.

  2. The advice he's pushing back on is the familiar "don't play by shapes, learn the intervals and the note names". His view is that it's good advice arriving at the wrong point in the process.

  3. First idea from the book: visual memory is stronger than auditory memory for most people. The passage he reads notes that although people assume music is especially memorable, the data doesn't support that for non-musicians, and that auditory memory is generally the weaker of the two.

  4. Second idea from the book: knowing and doing are different systems. Declarative knowledge — the book knowledge of what a C major scale is — is built for conscious recall. Procedural knowledge — actually playing it — is built for fast reflexes that don't have to pass through conscious thought. That's why the two can come apart so completely in a player.

  5. Put those together and you get a sequence. Lean into the guitar being a shape-based instrument: learn the shape by rote, get it under the fingers, and don't ask what the intervals are yet.

  6. "Seeing" means something specific here — being able to close your eyes and picture the shape instantly. Once that picture exists, layering root, fifth, seventh, ninth into the third on top of it is easy.

  7. The rule he never got on with: never play anything you haven't heard first. He found it an impossible standard, and notes that Julian Lage, asked whether he hears everything before playing it, said that isn't how he thinks about music either.

  8. When you're playing, the note-reading part of your brain doesn't need to be running. He can switch into seeing degrees when he wants to, but while playing he sees one large shape made of smaller boxes.

  9. The stop sign analogy. You can picture a stop sign without reading it, and spell out the letters later if you choose. Shapes work the same way: hold the picture, read it when there's a reason to.

  10. Melodies live inside shapes. Licks and tunes are pieces of scales, so once the scale is a clear picture, a new lick can be filed inside it — which is what makes it stay.

  11. His example is a jazz standard he learned at fifteen and can still play decades later, because he doesn't store it as a list of notes. He sees the scale it comes from, which also tells him instantly what key it's in, where the melody starts relative to the root, and where else on the neck he can take it.

  12. Why memorising feels so hard — abstract items with nothing to attach to. Shapes are the containers you attach them to.

  13. He ends by explicitly not making a rule of it. If you learn well by ear, or by note names, that's fine and you should carry on; he's offering what worked for him and his students, not a standard to be judged against.

Terminology​

TermWhat it means
Declarative knowledgeKnowing that — facts you can state: this chord is a maj7, this note is F♯
Procedural knowledgeKnowing how — the trained movement, running faster than conscious thought
Rote learningRepeating something until it sticks, before understanding it
Visual memoryRemembering what something looked like; unusually large capacity in humans
Auditory memoryRemembering what something sounded like; generally weaker, especially untrained
Chunk / containerA meaningful unit that several small facts can be stored inside
ShapeA fingering pattern held as a picture rather than as a list of notes
LayeringAdding the analysis — intervals, names, function — on top of a shape already learned
Neo-soul voicingsThe lush, extended chord shapes his student wanted; the anecdote's subject

Deep dive​

1. Why the fretboard resists being learned (0:00)​

The comparison to the piano is the same one he opens the chord routine video with, and it's fair: a keyboard displays its own structure, and a fretboard doesn't. Octaves, keys and intervals are visible on a piano before you know any theory. On a guitar the grid looks identical everywhere, so nothing about the layout tells you what you're looking at.

The consequence he draws is the interesting part. If the instrument won't supply structure visually, the player has to hold it in memory — so the question becomes which kind of memory, and that's what the rest of the video is about.

2. The two passages from the book (1:23)​

He's reading Guitar Zero with his community's book club and found two passages that together describe how he'd always taught, without having had a reason for it.

On memory. People tend to assume music is easy to remember, but the research doesn't support that for non-musicians, and auditory memory in general is substantially weaker than visual memory. His gloss: most of us aren't born musicians, so for most people seeing-and-remembering will beat hearing-and-remembering.

On two kinds of knowledge. Formal musical knowledge and practical skill come apart because the brain handles them differently. Declarative knowledge is built for conscious access; procedural skill is built for speed and doesn't need to pass through conscious thought. Hence the very common situation of a player who can explain a mode perfectly and can't use it, or the reverse.

Both of these are well-established outside music, which is worth knowing because it means the advice isn't just one teacher's preference:

  • Visual memory has a famously large capacity — people shown thousands of images can recognise them later at high accuracy, which is not true of arbitrary sounds.
  • The declarative/procedural split is standard in learning research. Riding a bike is the usual example: the knowledge that keeps you upright isn't available to introspection, and describing it doesn't help you do it.

3. The sequence that follows (4:13)​

The practical conclusion is an ordering, not a prohibition:

StepWhat you doWhat you're building
1Learn the shape by rote — "this is the shape, this is how I play it"a picture you can call up with your eyes shut
2Play it until it runs without supervisionprocedural skill
3Layer the analysis on: which string is the root, the 3rd, the 7thdeclarative knowledge, attached to something
4Connect it to what you already know: key, scale, where else it appearsthe container gets bigger

The anecdote that motivates it: a student who wanted a particular neo-soul voicing kept asking what the intervals were and where else he'd use it — before he could play the shape at all. The analysis isn't wrong, it's just early. There has to be something for it to stick to.

4. On "never play what you haven't heard first" (7:06)​

This is the part of the video with the most feeling in it, and it's aimed at a piece of advice most self-teaching players have run into.

His experience was that the rule set an impossible bar. He could close his eyes, see a shape, and know it was a minor scale or a dominant 7 — so in a dominant context, with the shape visible and reachable, inserting a "hear it first" step felt like an extra layer with no purpose. He mentions that Julian Lage, asked the same question, said hearing everything first isn't how he thinks about music either.

Two things worth separating, since both are true and they're often argued as if only one can be:

  • As a rule about what you're allowed to play, it blocks beginners. You can't pre-hear a sound you've never made. Something has to come first, and for most people that's the hands.
  • As a description of where you're heading, it's real. Players who can hear a line before playing it have something worth having, and the way they got it was by playing thousands of lines and listening to what came out.

Which makes it a destination rather than an entry requirement — and that's compatible with everything he's saying. He's clear himself that note names and the ear should be learned eventually.

5. What he actually sees while playing (8:51)​

A useful piece of self-observation. Playing a three-notes-per-string C major scale, he can deliberately switch into seeing degrees — root, fifth, sixth, fourth, third — but that's a mode he enters on purpose. While simply playing, he sees one large shape made of three-note boxes.

Then the stop sign: you can picture the sign without reading the letters, and spell them out if you want to. The picture and the reading are separate acts, and the picture is the faster one.

This is worth putting next to shapes vs names in these notes, which is about the cost of shape-thinking — what a shape quietly hides. The two fit together as sequence rather than disagreement: shapes get you playing and hold the material; the names are what stop the shapes from hiding things, and they're layered on when there's a shape to layer them onto.

6. Melodies stored inside shapes (10:15)​

He anticipates the objection that this is all information rather than music, and answers it with the strongest example in the video.

A jazz standard he learned at fifteen, still playable decades later without revision. When he plays it, he isn't recalling individual notes — he's seeing the scale the melody sits inside. Which gives him three things at once: the key, the melody's position relative to the root, and immediate access to the rest of the neck if he wants to improvise on it.

The mechanism has a name in learning research: chunking. The classic demonstration is with chess players, who can reconstruct a board after a few seconds' glance — but only if the position could have arisen in a real game. Given randomly scattered pieces, their recall drops to roughly a beginner's. The expertise isn't a better memory; it's having structures to store things in.

That's exactly his point about containers. A lick memorised as six unrelated notes has nowhere to live. The same lick understood as "this fragment, in this shape, in this key" is filed, and filing is what makes recall cheap twenty years later.

7. On teaching, and the disclaimer (13:14)​

He closes personally: he had good teachers and also teachers whose you must and you can't play unless made him a worse learner, and he's passionate about this because he'd have had an easier path if someone had framed it this way.

Then, plainly: this isn't a claim that learning by ear or by note names is wrong. If a different approach is working for you, keep doing it, and don't let anyone tell you your way of learning music is wrong.

That's a good note to hold onto when reading anything in this folder, including these pages.

What to pair it with​

  1. The ear, on its own schedule. Nothing here argues against ear training; it argues against making it a prerequisite. It still has to happen, and Hearing the Changes is where it starts.
  2. Actually doing step 3. The framework only pays off if the analysis eventually gets layered on. A shape you never read stays a shape — the stop sign is only useful because he can spell it.
  3. A test for whether a shape is really learned. Playing it isn't proof; being able to picture it away from the guitar is the standard he sets, and it's easy to check on a train or in a queue.
  4. Something to store. Containers need contents, which means real repertoire — Repertoire as Curriculum.

Practice​

There isn't an exercise in this video, so this is the framework turned into one. It works with whatever you're already learning.

StageWhat you doDone when
1Take one shape you're working on and play it daily without analysing itit runs without your supervision
2Away from the guitar, close your eyes and picture itthe picture arrives instantly, not by reconstruction
3Now name what each finger is playing — root, 3rd, 5th, 7thyou can say it while playing, without slowing down
4Find one lick or melody that lives inside that shapeyou can see the shape underneath the lick
5Work out what key it's in and where the melody starts relative to the rootyou could transpose it somewhere else on the neck

Stage 2 is the one people skip, and it's the one that costs nothing — it doesn't even need the instrument.
