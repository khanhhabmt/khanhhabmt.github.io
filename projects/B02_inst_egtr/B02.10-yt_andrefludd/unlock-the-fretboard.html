How To FINALLY Unlock The Guitar Fretboard

🎥 Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MSGfbN4wQow

Video: Andre Fludd — How To FINALLY Unlock The Guitar Fretboard · 23:34 · posted 26 Aug 2025 Subject: one navigation system built from two things you probably already own — barre chords and pentatonic boxes — with triads added as the targeting layer Key used: C major and its relative A minor Prerequisite he states outright: you need your barre chords first Format: this is a lesson from his course rather than a standalone tip video, so it assumes a little and refers forward to later material.

If you already know your five pentatonic shapes, this is the most directly useful video in the folder — because almost nothing in it is a new shape to memorise. It's a way of seeing shapes you already have.

The idea in one sentence​

Anchor on a barre chord you already know, and always see two more things on top of it — the pentatonic box for freedom and the triad for targeting — so that rhythm playing, soloing and nailing the changes all come out of one picture.

Summary of the ideas​

  1. Why guitarists talk about "fretboard visualisation" at all. Trumpet players don't discuss valve navigation and pianists don't discuss piano visualisation. The guitar needs it because its layout shows you nothing.

  2. The romantic goals need unpacking. "Nailing the changes", "playing freely", "playing what's in your head" get repeated as ambitions without anyone asking what they actually require of your hands.

  3. You're only ever playing two things: single notes belonging to some scale, or harmony — chords, triads, sevenths. (Technically a third: a dyad, two notes.)

  4. So the system needs only two tools: pentatonic scales and triads, in specific positions, tied to chords you already play.

  5. The barre chord is the anchor. Start from a shape you've played a thousand times, and hang everything else off it.

  6. Three layers, three jobs. The barre chord plays rhythm; the pentatonic box gives you freedom to improvise without wrong notes; the triad tells you where to land when the chord changes.

  7. Why the pentatonic is the "freedom" layer. Over a minor chord you might theoretically need Aeolian, Dorian, Phrygian, harmonic minor or melodic minor — five different scales to keep straight. The minor pentatonic sidesteps the choice, because it contains the notes those scales agree on.

  8. Why the triad is the "targeting" layer. Land on a triad note as the chord arrives and you've emphasised the chord, which is all "nailing the changes" means in practice.

  9. The habit to build: whenever you see that barre chord, automatically see the pentatonic box and the triad with it. Every time, until it's one object.

  10. Only three triad shapes exist. A triad has three notes, so it has three inversions — which he points out is fewer shapes than CAGED's five.

  11. Two of the three triads sit inside your two barre chords, and the third sits between them. For that middle one, with no barre chord to hang on, he anchors it by finding the root and the minor 3rd instead.

  12. He swaps one shape for a sweep-picking arpeggio. The pentatonic shape that would go with the middle triad reads as the relative major, so to keep the minor sound clear he uses the familiar minor arpeggio shape there instead.

  13. The same process covers major chords, and works over dominant chords too.

  14. Do this for major and minor and the pentatonic boxes appear by themselves — moving up the neck, the boxes alternate between the major triad and its relative minor.

Terminology​

TermWhat it means
AnchorA shape you already know well, used as the reference point for everything layered on it
Barre chordMovable chord shape with the first finger flat across the strings
TriadThree-note chord: root, 3rd, 5th
InversionThe same triad re-stacked so a different note is lowest; three notes means three inversions
Nailing the changesLanding on a note belonging to the chord at the moment it arrives

Deep dive​

1. Why this problem exists at all (0:01)​

The opening is a good reframe. Other instruments don't have a "navigation" literature because their layout carries information: a keyboard shows you where the octaves are before you know any theory. A fretboard is a uniform grid, so everything you know about it has to be carried in your head.

Then a sharper question: what are we navigating towards? He points out that guitarists repeat goals like nailing the changes or playing freely without defining them — and that once you define them, the practice follows. That's the whole method in one move: name the goal precisely, then build the smallest thing that produces it.

2. Everything you play is one of two things (1:44)​

Single notes from a scale, or harmony. That's the simplification the system rests on, and it's what lets two tools cover the whole neck.

3. The three layers (3:08)​

Take a minor barre chord you already play. Then, without moving your hand, add two more pictures on top of it:

LayerWhat it isWhat it's for
Barre chordthe shape you already knowrhythm playing; the anchor for the other two
Pentatonic boxthe box in that positionimprovising with no wrong notes
Triadthree notes from the chord, on the top stringslanding on the chord when it changes

He takes the triad from the D string to the high E string — the top part of the barre chord — because that's the register a solo tends to live in.

The instruction is simple and worth taking literally: every time you see that barre chord, see the other two as well. Not as three facts, as one object.

4. Why the pentatonic is the safety net (5:42)​

This is the strongest theoretical passage in the video, and it's worth doing the arithmetic behind it, because knowing why it's safe tells you exactly how far the safety extends.

Over a minor chord, which minor scale applies depends on context. Written as degrees — measured against the major scale of the same root, as explained in this aside:

Scale2nd3rd6th7th
Aeolian2♭3♭6♭7
Dorian2♭3♮6♭7
Phrygian♭2♭3♭6♭7
Harmonic minor2♭3♭6♮7
Melodic minor2♭3♮6♮7

Look down the columns and the disagreements are all in the 2nd, the 6th and the 7th. Now write out the minor pentatonic: 1, ♭3, 4, 5, ♭7. It contains no 2nd and no 6th at all — it simply doesn't own the notes the scales argue about. That's the mechanism: it's safe because it abstains.

The one column where it takes a side is the 7th, and it picks ♭7. So the net covers Aeolian, Dorian and Phrygian completely — the three you meet in nearly all pop, rock, blues and most jazz minor situations. In music that leans on harmonic or melodic minor, where the ♮7 is the whole point of the sound, the ♭7 is the one note to keep short. Useful to know, and it doesn't dent the trick at all: the overwhelming majority of minor chords you'll play are covered.

5. Why the triad layer costs almost nothing (7:53)​

Here's the part worth pointing out to anyone who already knows their pentatonic shapes. The triad isn't a fourth thing to learn — it's already inside the box.

A minor pentatonic is A, C, D, E, G. The A minor triad is A, C, E. All three are in the scale you already play. The pentatonic is the triad plus the 4th and the ♭7.

So "adding the triad layer" means learning to see three of the five notes you already know differently. And that difference is the entire distinction between wandering around a box and nailing a change: the same shape, with three of its notes marked as arrival points.

This is the same idea as Chord Tones on the Neck in these notes — the arpeggio lives inside the box, and your hand doesn't have to go anywhere.

6. Three shapes, not five (11:36)​

His argument for simplicity: a triad has three notes, so there are three inversions and therefore three shapes to know per quality. Two of them line up with the two barre chords you already use, and the third sits between them.

For that middle shape there's no barre chord to hang it on, so he anchors it by interval instead: find the root — which you know from the barre chord positions — then the minor 3rd above it, which also happens to be the second note of the pentatonic box. From there the triad shape falls out.

That's a small but transferable technique: when a shape has no chord to attach to, attach it to an interval you can already find.

7. The one substitution (13:43)​

Honest and worth understanding rather than just copying. The pentatonic shape that would pair with the middle triad is also pattern one of the relative major pentatonic, and the triad in that spot can be heard as a major 6 chord. In an A minor song it still functions as A minor, but the picture is ambiguous.

Rather than fight the ambiguity, he swaps in the standard minor arpeggio shape — the sweep-picking one most players already have in their hands — because it states the minor sound plainly and connects the two barre chord positions.

The reason for the ambiguity is worth a sentence, since it explains something that confuses a lot of players: C major pentatonic and A minor pentatonic are the same five notes. C-D-E-G-A and A-C-D-E-G are one set. Nothing in the shape can tell you which one you're in — only the music underneath can. It's the same point as The Tonal Centre, showing up as a practical fingering decision.

8. Major chords, and dominant too (17:30)​

The same three-layer treatment applied to C major: barre chord, pentatonic shape, triad, for each position. He notes that one of the major shapes overlaps with a minor pentatonic shape learned earlier — same grid of notes, different root — and that the whole package also works over a dominant chord.

9. Why the boxes then appear on their own (21:31)​

The observation he ends with: run this through major and minor and you find you've generated nearly every pentatonic box, alternating between the major triad and its relative minor as you move up the neck.

That follows from the same fact as the substitution above. Since C major pentatonic and A minor pentatonic are one set of notes, each box contains both a C major triad and an A minor triad. Which one you see depends on which chord is playing. So you're not learning two systems of boxes — you're learning one set of boxes and two ways of reading each.

He skips one box that "doesn't fit perfectly" and moves on, which is fair for an overview lesson.

What to pair it with​

  1. Barre chords, if they're not solid. He says this outright at the start, and he's right — the whole system hangs off them.
  2. A backing track or loop with an actual chord change. The triad layer only proves itself when a chord arrives and you land on it. Without changes, the pentatonic layer alone will feel like enough, and you'll skip the part that matters.
  3. Guide Tones as the next step up. Triad targeting says land on a chord tone; guide tones say which chord tone carries the most information.
  4. Something to play it on. A song with two or three chords is worth more here than another exercise.

Practice​

If you already know your pentatonic boxes, this is mostly re-seeing rather than learning, so it goes faster than it looks.

StageWhat you doDone when
1Hold one minor barre chord, then play the pentatonic box in that position without moving your handthe two feel like the same place
2Inside that box, find the three triad notes and play only thoseyou can pick them out without hunting
3Improvise in the box over a one-chord loop, landing on a triad note every four barsthe landings sound like arrivals, not accidents
4Add the second barre chord position and its box and triadyou can move between the two positions mid-phrase
5Loop two chords and land on each chord's triad note as it arrivesthe change is audible in your line, not just in the backing

Stage 5 is the goal of the whole video. Everything before it is preparation.
