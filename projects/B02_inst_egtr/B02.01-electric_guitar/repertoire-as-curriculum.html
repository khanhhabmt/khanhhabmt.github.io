Repertoire as Curriculum

Self-teaching players usually treat songs as the reward for practice: do the boring drills, then play something fun. That ordering is backwards and it's why so many self-taught players have good technique and no music.

The better frame:

Songs are the curriculum, not the prize. Every technique you need already exists inside music you want to play. Choose the songs deliberately and they will select your drills for you.

This solves the hardest problem in self-teaching, which is not knowing what to learn next — the one thing a teacher genuinely provides that a feedback loop doesn't. A well-chosen song is a curriculum you didn't have to design.

1. Choosing songs that teach​

Three criteria, in order:

(1) Slightly beyond you. The song should contain two or three things you can't currently do — not twenty. If you can already play all of it, it's repertoire maintenance. If none of it is reachable, you'll spend six weeks and quit.

(2) It contains a target skill. Pick songs because of what they'll force. Choosing by what technique it will teach turns listening into curriculum design:

need muting practice?        → anything palm-muted and rhythmic
need bends and vibrato?      → blues, classic rock lead lines
need chord-change speed?     → up-tempo pop/rock rhythm parts
need timing?                 → funk, anything syncopated
need fretboard knowledge?    → something that moves positions

(3) You actually like it. Non-negotiable and frequently violated. You will play this hundreds of times. Learning a song you find boring because it's "good for technique" wastes the one advantage self-teaching has — you get to choose.

A reasonable load is two songs at once: one slightly hard (the project) and one easy (the one you can enjoy). Any more than three and you're in failure mode B from file 02.

2. Breaking a song down​

Don't start at the beginning and play until it falls apart. That practises the intro forty times and the hard part twice.

1. LISTEN  first, without the guitar. Learn how it goes.
2. MAP     the sections: intro / verse / chorus / solo. Note which repeat.
3. TRIAGE  mark each section: [can play] [nearly] [can't]
4. ATTACK  the [can't] sections FIRST, with the practice loop from file 05
5. ASSEMBLE only once the hard parts stand alone

Step 3 is the one people skip, and it's where the leverage is. Most songs are 80% material you can already handle plus a few genuinely hard bars. Those bars are the song. Practising the rest is just enjoying yourself with extra steps — pleasant, but book it under Spark.

Step 5 has its own difficulty worth naming: passages that work in isolation often break at the seams. Practise the transitions — the last beat of the easy section into the first beat of the hard one — as their own isolated units.

3. Playing along, and what it fixes​

Practising with the original recording does something practising alone cannot: it supplies an external clock and an external reference simultaneously. You find out immediately if you're rushing, if the feel is wrong, if you've learned the rhythm subtly incorrectly.

This is where a practice amp's playback features stop being conveniences and become methodology. Using the stem-separation-style tools now common on modeling amps:

ModeWhat it gives you
Full mixreference — hear how your part fits
Guitar removedyour part is now your job; nothing to hide behind
Drums onlythe song as a pure timing exercise, no harmonic crutch
Guitar isolatedstudy the original part closely — attack, vibrato, feel

The progression is a genuine difficulty ladder, and it's worth walking deliberately:

play along with full mix   → easy, you're doubled and hidden
play with guitar removed   → exposed; every error is audible
play with drums only       → hardest; you supply all harmonic content

Most players never leave step one, because it sounds best. Step two is where the learning is — with the original guitar muted, there's nothing masking your timing or your muting, and the arrangement has a hole exactly your size.

4. Learning by ear​

Tab is a fine shortcut and a poor teacher. It gives you the notes and none of the information that makes the part sound like the record — attack, timing feel, which notes are muted, where the vibrato goes.

Working some material out by ear is the highest-value skill in this file, and it compounds across everything else:

start absurdly small:  the bass note of each chord — that's it
then:                  the chord quality — major or minor?
then:                  a two-note melodic fragment
then:                  a full riff

Two minutes a day of this is enough to change your ear over months. The move that makes it tractable: sing the note first, then find it. Singing forces you to identify the pitch internally; hunting for it on the fretboard without that step is guessing.

Expect this to be slow and frustrating early. It's the one skill here with no shortcut, and the one that most separates players who can only reproduce from players who can participate.

5. Finishing things​

A specific discipline, because self-taught players are notorious for a repertoire of intros.

Define "done" in advance. A workable standard:

I can play it start to finish, at tempo, along with the record, twice in a row, without stopping — and I have a recording of it.

That's demanding, and it should be. Note what it excludes: playing it nearly right, or playing it right once out of eight attempts.

Then keep it. Maintain a small list of finished songs and cycle through them occasionally. This matters for three reasons: it's the only real evidence of progress, finished songs are what you can actually play for another human, and revisiting old material shows you how much easier it has become — which is the most motivating feedback available.

Finishing beats collecting. Three songs you can play completely are worth more than thirty you can start — musically, motivationally, and as proof to yourself that the routine works.

6. Takeaway​

  • Songs are the curriculum, not the reward. Chosen well, they select your drills and solve the "what next?" problem a teacher usually solves.
  • Choose on three criteria: slightly beyond you, contains a target skill, and you genuinely like it. Two projects at a time, three maximum.
  • Break down by listen → map → triage → attack the hard parts first → assemble. The few hard bars are the song; practise the seams too.
  • Play along with the record. Ladder: full mix → guitar removed → drums only. Most players never leave step one; step two is where the learning is.
  • Learn some material by ear, starting absurdly small. Sing it, then find it. Slow, frustrating, and the highest-compounding skill here.
  • Define "done" in advance (start to finish, at tempo, with the record, twice, recorded) and keep a finished list. Finishing beats collecting.
