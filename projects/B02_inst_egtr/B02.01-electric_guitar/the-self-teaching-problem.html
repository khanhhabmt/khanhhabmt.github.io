The Self-Teaching Problem

The reason self-teaching is hard is not lack of information. Information is abundant, free, and better than what most teachers could give you in 2005. If information were the constraint, the internet would have solved guitar.

The actual constraint:

A teacher is an error-detection system. Without one, you practice your mistakes with perfect discipline and they become permanent.

This file is about building a replacement out of things you already own.

1. Why you cannot hear yourself​

Three separate mechanisms conspire, and knowing them makes the fixes obvious.

(a) Attention is single-threaded. While playing, nearly all of your attention is on producing the next note. There is very little left for evaluating the one you just played. This is why problems that are glaring on a recording are inaudible while you play — you were busy.

(b) You hear your intention, not your output. Your brain knows what it told your hands to do, and it helpfully overwrites the audio with that expectation. Small timing errors and buzzes get edited out before they reach consciousness.

(c) Errors hide behind gain and volume. Distortion compresses and smears; loud volume masks detail. The more flattering your tone, the less you can hear.

None of these is a discipline failure. They're structural, and structural problems need structural fixes rather than trying harder.

2. The replacement: four instruments of feedback​

Recording — the non-negotiable one​

Recording yourself is the single highest-leverage habit available to a self-teaching player, and almost nobody does it enough because the first few times are unpleasant.

The unpleasantness is the value: the gap between how you thought you sounded and how you sound is exactly the error signal you're missing. Playback moves you from performer to listener, which frees the attention that (a) was consuming.

minimum viable version:
  phone voice memo, once a week, one thing you're working on, 30 seconds

That is enough to change your trajectory. A weekly archive also gives you the only honest measure of progress — see file 08.

A reference — something to be wrong against​

Detecting an error requires knowing what correct sounds like. Play along with the original recording rather than practicing in isolation, and errors announce themselves: you'll drift, land early, or fall out of the pocket, and you'll hear it happen.

This is why playing along with records beats playing alone, and why a practice tool that isolates or removes parts is worth real money.

Slowness — buying attention back​

Playing slowly is not "the same thing, easier." It's an attention purchase: at half speed, the fraction of your mind spent on production drops and the remainder becomes available for evaluation. You start hearing your own buzzes and uneven notes in real time.

This is the same argument as slow-practice psychology in the drum section — it generalizes across instruments because the bottleneck is attention, not fingers.

A clean tone — removing the anesthetic​

Distortion is flattering; that's its job. Practicing technique on a clean or low-gain setting is uncomfortable in exactly the way that's useful. Every buzz, every unmuted string, every uneven pick attack is audible.

Rule of thumb: learn it clean, then play it dirty. More in file 04.

3. Your practice rig already does most of this​

Worth mapping deliberately, because a practice amp bought for convenience often turns out to contain the entire feedback apparatus. Taking the JBL BandBox Solo as the example — 18 W RMS through a single 2.25″ full-range driver, with amp and effect models on board:

Feedback needThe feature that serves it
Reference to play againstBluetooth streaming — play along with the record, not alone
Isolating a partStem AI separation — mute the original guitar and take its place, or solo the drums for a bare-bones backing track
Timing error detectionBuilt-in metronome
Pitch error detectionBuilt-in tuner
Recording yourselfUSB audio out (48 kHz / 24-bit) straight into a DAW or phone
Quiet practiceHeadphone out, so late-night sessions stay possible
Removing friction~6 h battery — the amp can live where you actually sit

Two of those deserve emphasis.

Stem separation is a genuine pedagogical tool, not a gimmick. Removing the guitar from a finished record turns any song you like into a backing track with a guitar-shaped hole in it. That gives you a reference and forces you to fill the space — the closest thing to playing in a band that exists alone in a room. Muting everything but drums gives you the same song as a timing exercise.

Full-range is unflattering, and that's a feature. A 2.25″ full-range driver does not have the mid-focused voicing and high-end rolloff of a real guitar speaker, so it won't sound like a recording — but it will let through pick noise, fret buzz, and string squeak that a guitar speaker would soften. For detecting errors, honesty beats flattery. Just don't judge your tone by it.

Where it's weak, honestly: at 18 W through a small driver there's no real low end and no power-amp feel, so it's a practice and detection tool, not a tone-reference or a band amp. (Marketing says "up to 30 W"; the published RMS figure is 18 W — the gap is the usual peak-vs-RMS accounting.) None of that matters for what this section uses it for.

4. The two ways self-teaching fails​

Almost every stalled self-taught player is in one of these two states. They have opposite symptoms and opposite fixes, so diagnose before treating.

Failure mode A — the comfortable plateau. You practice regularly, and you practice what you can already do. Sessions feel good and nothing improves. This is the more common one, and it feels like virtue.

Tell: you can play your practice material well. Real practice should sound bad most of the time — you should be operating at the edge where failure is frequent.

Fix: deliberately work on material you cannot yet play, at a tempo where you fail. See The Practice Loop.

Failure mode B — the grasshopper. You start many things and finish none. Ten riff intros, no complete songs, a new technique every week. Feels productive, consolidates nothing.

Tell: you can start a dozen songs and finish zero.

Fix: hard cap on simultaneous projects (two or three), and a definition of "done" you commit to in advance. See Repertoire as Curriculum.

A useful weekly question: "what could I not do last week that I can do now?" If there's no answer for several weeks running, you're in A or B.

5. What a teacher still does better​

Honesty requires saying what this doesn't replace. Even with a perfect feedback loop, a teacher beats self-study at:

  • Spotting physical problems you can't see — wrist angle, tension, posture. These cause injuries, and injuries end hobbies. Worth a handful of lessons purely for this, even if you self-teach otherwise.
  • Curriculum ordering — knowing what to learn next, which is hard to derive when you don't yet know what you don't know.
  • Calibration — telling you whether "pretty good" is actually good.

A reasonable compromise: self-teach as the default, and buy an occasional lesson as an audit — every couple of months, to check technique and re-aim. That converts the teacher from a weekly expense into a periodic diagnostic, which is where their value is highest anyway.

6. Takeaway​

  • The constraint on self-teaching is error detection, not information.
  • You can't hear yourself because attention is single-threaded, you hear your intention, and gain masks detail. Structural problems, structural fixes.
  • Four instruments of feedback: record yourself, play against a reference, slow down to buy attention, practice clean.
  • A modeling practice amp usually contains most of that apparatus already — metronome, tuner, USB recording, and stem separation to turn any record into a backing track with your part removed.
  • Two failure modes: the comfortable plateau (practicing what you can already do) and the grasshopper (starting everything, finishing nothing).
  • Keep a teacher for what a feedback loop can't do: physical/injury diagnosis, curriculum ordering, and calibration.
