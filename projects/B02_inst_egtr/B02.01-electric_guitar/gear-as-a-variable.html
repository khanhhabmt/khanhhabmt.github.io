Gear as a Variable

Gear occupies a strange place in guitar culture: it is simultaneously over-discussed as a source of tone and under-understood as a practice variable. Both errors come from the same mistake — treating gear as something you buy rather than something you set.

The framing that makes it useful:

Your gear settings determine what your technique has to do, and what your mistakes sound like. They are practice parameters, like tempo.

This file is deliberately about principles rather than products. Specific models date; gain staging doesn't.

1. The chain, and where each link acts​

strings ─► pickups ─► [effects] ─► preamp/gain ─► tone stack ─► power ─► speaker ─► room
   │          │                        │              │                     │
 attack   what gets            how much it        which frequencies    what survives
 & pitch  heard at all           clips             get through          to your ear

Two properties of this chain do most of the practical work:

It's serial. Anything a link removes cannot be recovered downstream. Notes lost to bad muting arrive at the amp already lost. Gear cannot add information that wasn't in your hands — which is the true, non-mystical version of "tone is in the fingers."

Order matters. The same effects in a different order sound different, because each one processes what the previous one produced. Distortion before reverb sounds like a guitar in a room; reverb before distortion sounds like a distorted room.

2. Gain: the single most important practice parameter​

Gain is how hard you drive one stage into clipping. Everything people associate with "electric guitar sound" follows from it.

What gain actually does:

  • Clips the waveform, adding harmonic content — this is the "distortion."
  • Compresses, squashing the difference between soft and loud.
  • Increases sustain, because a decaying note stays above the clipping threshold longer.
  • Raises the noise floor and makes everything ring, including strings you aren't playing.

What gain hides, and what it exposes​

This is the part that matters for practice, and it's not symmetric:

More gain hidesMore gain exposes
uneven picking attackunmuted strings and string noise
weak or inconsistent volumesloppy note releases
thin tone, poor pick controlsympathetic ringing
small timing inconsistenciesany left-hand movement noise

So high gain is flattering for articulation and merciless for noise. Which gives two opposite diagnostic uses:

practice CLEAN     → to find articulation problems (evenness, buzz, attack)
practice HIGH GAIN → to find noise problems (muting, releases)

Most players do neither: they sit at medium gain permanently, where everything is moderately hidden. Default to clean for technique work, then deliberately crank the gain occasionally as a muting audit.

The general rule, worth making a habit: learn it clean, then play it dirty.

3. Gain staging: where the clipping happens​

"Gain" is not one control. Several stages can clip, and which one clips determines the character:

pickup output  →  drive/gain knob  →  master volume  →  speaker
   (hot pickups    (preamp clipping:    (power stage:     (physical
    push harder)    fizzy, tight,        looser, warmer,   limits)
                    controllable)        needs volume)

The practical version for a small practice amp: you're almost always hearing preamp clipping, because you can't push a power stage at living-room volume. That's fine — just know that "why doesn't it sound like the record" is often this, not your playing.

Two useful consequences:

  • The guitar's volume knob is a gain control. Rolling it down reduces what hits the preamp, cleaning up the distortion without changing the amp. This is a live performance technique — dirty for the chorus, roll back for the verse — and it's free.
  • Pickup choice changes gain. A higher-output pickup drives the front end harder, which is why a humbucker sounds more distorted than a single coil into an identical setting. Position matters too: neck pickup = warmer/rounder, bridge pickup = brighter/more aggressive.

4. Amp models and effects, briefly​

A modeling amp gives you many voicings; the risk is spending practice time browsing them. A minimal working taxonomy:

CategoryExamplesWhat it's for
Gainclean, crunch, high gainthe fundamental character
Modulationchorus, phaser, tremolomovement and width
Timedelay, reverbspace and depth
Dynamicscompressionevenness (and hiding unevenness)

Standard order is roughly gain → modulation → time, which is why reverb usually sits last.

A discipline worth adopting early: pick three sounds and stay there. One clean, one medium crunch, one high gain. Preset-browsing is not practice, and a player who knows three sounds deeply makes better records than one who knows forty shallowly.

Note also that compression is ambiguous for a learner. It genuinely helps sustain and evenness — and it also masks exactly the inconsistency you're trying to detect. Keep it off while working on element 2 from The Elements.

5. Setup: the part that's actually worth attention​

Unlike effects, setup changes how the instrument plays, and a bad setup will make you believe you're bad at something you're not.

AdjustmentWhat it changesSymptom when wrong
Action (string height)effort to frettoo high: fatigue, sharp notes. too low: buzz
Intonation (saddle position)in-tuneness up the neckopen chords fine, 12th-fret chords sour
Pickup heightoutput and balanceone pickup much louder; too close = warbly
String gaugebendability, tone, tensionheavy strings make bends hard for a beginner
Neck relief (truss rod)curvaturebuzz in specific neck regions

Intonation deserves specific attention because it's the one that makes you doubt your ears. If the guitar plays in tune in open position and sour at the 12th fret, that's a hardware problem, not a technique problem — and it's exactly the compensation issue derived in Guitar Frets & Compensation.

Coming from acoustic, one specific recommendation: start with light strings (9s or 10s). Acoustic players habitually pick heavier gauges out of familiarity, and heavy strings make bends and vibrato — two of your three priority skills — substantially harder to learn.

6. The honest part​

Three claims that get argued about, resolved as plainly as possible:

"Tone is in the hands." Mostly true and slightly dishonest. Your hands determine attack, vibrato, dynamics, and note choice — the identifiable part of a player's sound. But the amp determines the gain structure and frequency response, and no amount of technique makes a clean amp sound distorted. Hands own expression; gear owns character.

"Better gear makes you sound better." True for character, false for skill. New gear changes what your errors sound like, not how many you make. The reliable observation: players who upgrade to fix a problem usually still have it.

"Practice on what you'll perform on." Sensible in principle, impractical at home. A small practice amp misrepresents low end and power-amp behaviour — so use it for detection and don't judge your tone by it. Just be aware which of your judgments the tool is qualified to make.

The bottom line for a self-teaching player: gear is a variable you should learn to set deliberately, not a problem you should try to buy your way out of. Set it to make your current mistakes as audible as possible, and it becomes a teaching tool. Set it to make you sound good, and it becomes an anesthetic.

7. Takeaway​

  • The chain is serial: gear can't restore what your hands didn't produce. Effect order changes the result.
  • Gain clips, compresses, sustains, and raises noise. It hides articulation problems and exposes noise problems — so practice clean for articulation, high-gain as a muting audit. Learn it clean, play it dirty.
  • The guitar's volume knob is a gain control, and pickup choice/position changes how hard the front end is driven.
  • Pick three sounds and stay there. Preset browsing isn't practice. Compression masks the unevenness you're trying to hear.
  • Setup matters more than effects. Bad intonation makes you doubt your ears; from acoustic, start with light strings so bends stay learnable.
  • Hands own expression, gear owns character. Set gear to make mistakes audible, not to make yourself sound good.
