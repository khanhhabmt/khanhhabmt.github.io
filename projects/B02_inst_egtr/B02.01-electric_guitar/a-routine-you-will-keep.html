A Routine You Will Keep

The best practice routine is not the optimal one. It's the one still running in six months.

This deserves stating plainly because the failure mode is so predictable: an adult learner designs a beautiful 90-minute daily schedule, runs it for nine days, misses one, feels guilty, misses three more, and quits. The routine didn't fail because it was wrong — it failed because it was fragile.

Design for the bad week, not the good one.

1. Consistency beats duration, by a lot​

Skill consolidates between sessions rather than during them. That single fact sets the entire design:

20 minutes × 6 days   =  2 hours    → six consolidation cycles
2 hours   × 1 day     =  2 hours    → one consolidation cycle

Same total time; the first produces substantially more skill. Frequency is the variable that matters, and it's also the cheaper one to protect — twenty minutes survives a busy week, ninety doesn't.

For a self-teaching adult with a job, the practical target is short and daily, not long and scheduled.

2. The minimum viable session​

The mechanism that actually keeps a routine alive: define a session so small you cannot justify skipping it.

MVS = 10 minutes.  Guitar out, amp on, one thing practised properly.

Rules that make it work:

  • The MVS counts as a full session. Not a partial one, not a failure. If you do ten minutes, the day is a success. This is the whole trick — it removes the all-or-nothing logic that kills routines.
  • Most days you'll do more. Starting is the expensive part; once the guitar is in your hands, twenty or forty minutes usually happens on its own. But the commitment stays at ten.
  • On bad days, ten is enough. The point isn't the ten minutes of practice. It's that the streak survives, and the identity survives with it.

Remove friction ruthlessly. A guitar in a case in a cupboard is a guitar you won't play. Guitar on a stand, amp within reach, cable already connected — the setup time between "I could practise" and "I am practising" should be under ten seconds. A battery-powered practice amp is genuinely useful here for exactly this unglamorous reason: it can live wherever you actually sit.

3. Two streams: the Grind and the Spark​

The drum section splits practice into The Grind (technical, syllabus, metronome work) and The Spark (songs, improvisation, gear, fun). That split is worth borrowing wholesale, because it solves a problem neither stream solves alone.

StreamWhat it isWhat it's forFailure if it's the only stream
Grindelement drills, the practice loop, timing workactual skill growthyou quit — it's not fun
Sparklearning songs, jamming, playing alongmotivation, musical contextyou plateau — no targeted work

The two are not in competition; each is the other's insurance. The Grind without the Spark burns out. The Spark without the Grind is failure mode A from file 02 — pleasant, endless, static.

A working ratio is roughly 70% Grind / 30% Spark on a normal day, with the Spark deliberately placed last so sessions end well. On a ten-minute MVS day, Spark-only is fine — keeping the habit alive is the goal.

Worth noting: the Spark is also where the point lives. You didn't buy an electric guitar to do muting drills.

4. What to actually put in a week​

A concrete allocation, given the acoustic-player starting point from file 03:

EVERY session (Grind)
  · 5 min   noise control drill          ← the priority element
  · 10 min  the practice loop on ONE hard unit

ROTATE (Grind) — one per session
  · fretting-hand pressure / economy
  · picking evenness
  · bends to a target pitch, vibrato
  · fretboard note-naming
  · timing with sparse click

EVERY session (Spark)
  · 5-10 min  the song you're currently learning

WEEKLY
  · record 30 seconds of the current project     → file 08
  · one deliberate listening session (no guitar) → file 07

The rotation matters: you can't drill seven elements every day, but you can touch each one every few days, which is enough for all of them to move.

5. Things that quietly wreck routines​

Five specific traps, all common:

Practising only when motivated. Motivation is an output of practice more often than an input. The MVS exists precisely to make the routine independent of how you feel.

Making up missed sessions. Doubling tomorrow to "catch up" turns a small miss into a large obligation, which gets skipped, which ends the routine. A missed day is gone; just do today's session.

Tracking too much. An elaborate spreadsheet is procrastination wearing a productivity costume. A date, a tempo, and one line of what you worked on is enough.

Practising tired, late, at the end of everything. Guitar scheduled for 11pm after everything else has claimed your attention will be the first thing cut. Attach it to an existing anchor in your day instead — before dinner, after the commute — so it doesn't compete for a decision each time.

Buying gear when progress stalls. Reliable and expensive. See file 04: new gear changes what your errors sound like, not how many you make.

6. Expect plateaus, and don't respond to them​

Progress is not linear. Long stretches of no apparent improvement, followed by sudden jumps, is the normal shape of motor skill acquisition — not a sign the routine is broken.

The correct response to a plateau is keep going and change nothing for a few weeks. The incorrect responses are all tempting: overhaul the routine, buy something, switch to a different style, conclude you lack talent.

That said, distinguish a plateau from failure mode A. If you've been practising comfortable material for two months, that's not a plateau — that's rehearsal, and it needs a real change. The diagnostic is the weekly question from file 02: what can I do now that I couldn't last month? A plateau has an answer that's small. Rehearsal has no answer at all.

7. Takeaway​

  • Design for the bad week. The best routine is the one still running in six months, not the optimal one.
  • Frequency beats duration — skill consolidates between sessions. 20 min × 6 days ≫ 2 hours × 1 day.
  • Define a 10-minute minimum viable session that counts as a full session. It removes all-or-nothing logic; most days you'll do more anyway.
  • Remove friction: guitar on a stand, amp plugged in, under ten seconds to start.
  • Run Grind (70%) + Spark (30%), Spark last so sessions end well. Each stream is the other's insurance.
  • Routine-killers: practising only when motivated, making up missed sessions, over-tracking, late-night scheduling, buying gear at a plateau.
  • Plateaus are normal — keep going, change nothing. But distinguish them from rehearsing comfortable material, which needs a real change.
