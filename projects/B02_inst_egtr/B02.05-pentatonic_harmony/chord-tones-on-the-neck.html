Chord Tones on the Neck

You know where the pentatonic is. This page puts the chords on top of it, in the same window, so that "playing over the changes" becomes a matter of selecting rather than moving.

The arpeggio lives inside the box. Your hand doesn't go anywhere — your attention does.

That's the practical claim, and the diagrams below are the evidence. Everything is frets 5–9, which is box 1 plus one fret.

●   root of this chord     ○   another tone of this chord
·   pentatonic note that is NOT in this chord

Watch the · symbols. Those are notes that were fine a bar ago and are still fine — just no longer arrivals. The shifting pattern of ●○ against · is the harmony moving, drawn.

1. The four shapes​

Am — A C E​

   e  │──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──·──┼─────│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──·──┼─────┼──○──│
   D  │──·──┼─────┼──●──┼─────┼─────│
   A  │──·──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼─────│
   E  │──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
         5     6     7     8     9

Nine chord tones out of twelve pentatonic positions — and nothing outside the pentatonic. This is the home chord, and the scale was built on it.

F — F A C​

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   B  │──·──┼──●──┼─────┼──·──┼─────│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──·──┼─────┼──·──│
   D  │──·──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼─────│
   A  │──·──┼─────┼──·──┼──●──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
         5     6     7     8     9

The two ● at B/6 and A/8 are the note F — outside the box, one fret off the pattern you know. That single fret is the difference between implying the chord and stating it.

C — C E G​

   e  │──·──┼─────┼─────┼──●──┼─────│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   G  │──●──┼─────┼──·──┼─────┼──○──│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──·──┼─────┼─────│
   A  │──·──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼─────│
   E  │──·──┼─────┼─────┼──●──┼─────│
         5     6     7     8     9

Like Am, entirely inside the pentatonic. Note how different the pattern looks from Am even though both use only box notes — that difference is exactly the relative major/minor shift from The Tonal Centre, now visible as geometry.

G — G B D​

   e  │──·──┼─────┼──○──┼──·──┼─────│
   B  │──·──┼─────┼─────┼──●──┼─────│
   G  │──·──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼──·──│
   D  │──●──┼─────┼──·──┼─────┼──○──│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──·──┼─────┼─────│
   E  │──·──┼─────┼──○──┼──·──┼─────│
         5     6     7     8     9

Three of its seven tones — e/7, E/7, D/9 — are the note B, outside the pentatonic. This is the sparsest of the four and the most transformed by one added note.

2. What the four diagrams say together​

The chord that changes most is G. Compare the · count: Am and C are mostly chord tones, F and G are mostly ·. That asymmetry is why the progression has a shape at all — two stable bars and two moving ones.

One fret does a lot. F needs B/6 or A/8; G needs E/7, e/7 or D/9. Four positions total, all within one fret of something you already know. That is the entire cost of playing the changes instead of the key.

Your hand does not move. Every diagram is the same five-fret window. What changes is which cells you treat as home.

3. Drill 3.1 · Arpeggio only​

For each chord, play only its chord tones, ascending and descending, over a loop of that one chord. No pentatonic notes, no passing tones.

Two minutes each. Start with Am and C since they need nothing new, then F and G.

This sounds mechanical and is meant to — you're building the ability to see the chord inside the box, which is a visual skill before it's a musical one.

Test: play an arpeggio with your eyes closed and don't hit a ·.

4. Drill 3.2 · The switch​

Loop Am for four bars, then C for four bars. Play arpeggios only, and do not move your hand.

The whole exercise is noticing that the shape reorganizes underneath a stationary hand. Then do Am → F and C → G, which additionally require the one outside note.

Test: eight cycles with no hand movement and no wrong tones.

5. Drill 3.3 · Chord tones on the beat​

Now put the pentatonic back in, with one rule:

On beats 1 and 3, play a chord tone. Between them, play whatever you like.

This is the practical form of everything on the page, and it's how the skill actually gets used. You are not restricted to arpeggios in real playing — you're using arpeggios to place the strong beats and the scale to fill the gaps.

The · notes are now doing their real job: connective tissue. They were never wrong, they're just not where you land.

Test: four bars of Am F C G where every beat 1 and beat 3 is a chord tone, at a slow tempo, without hunting.

6. Drill 3.4 · Say what you're playing​

Play slowly over the loop and name each note's function, not its name:

"root ... third ... fifth ... passing ... root"

This is the harmonic version of the naming drill from Box One, and it does the same job: it forces retrieval of meaning rather than position. A note's name is a fact about the neck; its function is a fact about the music, and function is what transfers to other keys and other songs.

Test: over F, correctly identify A as the third and C as the fifth without counting.

Takeaway​

  • The arpeggio lives inside the box. Your hand stays; your attention moves.
  • Am and C are entirely inside the pentatonic. F needs F; G needs B — four positions total, all one fret from something you know.
  • The · notes aren't wrong, they're demoted — connective tissue rather than arrivals. The shifting ●○ against · is the harmony, drawn.
  • G is the sparsest chord and the one most transformed by one added note, which is why the progression has two stable bars and two moving ones.
  • The usable rule: chord tone on beats 1 and 3, anything in between.
  • Name functions, not note names — function transfers to other keys.
