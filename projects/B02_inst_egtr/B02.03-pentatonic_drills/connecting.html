Connecting

Boxes are not the difficult part. The joins are. Phrases break where you change position, not in the middle of a shape, and almost nobody practises the joins as their own thing.

There's a structural fact that makes this much easier than it sounds:

Adjacent boxes overlap. The notes they share are the seam.

You don't have to invent a way across. The crossing point is already written into the geometry.

Drill 3.1 · Box 1 into Box 2​

Box 1 is frets 5–8. Box 2 is frets 7–10. Here they are on one strip of neck, with their spans bracketed underneath:

   e  │─●─┼───┼───┼─○─┼───┼─○─│
   B  │─○─┼───┼───┼─○─┼───┼─●─│
   G  │─○─┼───┼─○─┼───┼─○─┼───│
   D  │─○─┼───┼─●─┼───┼───┼─○─│
   A  │─○─┼───┼─○─┼───┼───┼─○─│
   E  │─●─┼───┼───┼─○─┼───┼─○─│
        5   6   7   8   9  10
        ◆       ◆       ◆
       └────box 1────┘
               └────box 2────┘

The overlap is frets 7–8, and it contains exactly one shared note per string:

   e/8 = C      B/8 = G      G/7 = D
   D/7 = A      A/7 = E      E/8 = C

Every one of those is the highest note of box 1 on that string and the lowest note of box 2 on that string. That is the door.

The drill: play up box 1. When you reach the shared note on any string, stay on it, shift your hand into box 2 position, and continue up. Come back the same way.

Test: ascend from box 1 through box 2 and back, in time, with no pause at the shift.

Drill 3.2 · Box 5 into Box 1​

Going down instead. Box 5 also sits at frets 2–5 (the neck repeats every twelve frets), so it leads directly into box 1:

   e  │───┼─○─┼───┼─●─┼───┼───┼─○─│
   B  │───┼─○─┼───┼─○─┼───┼───┼─○─│
   G  │─●─┼───┼───┼─○─┼───┼─○─┼───│
   D  │─○─┼───┼───┼─○─┼───┼─●─┼───│
   A  │───┼─○─┼───┼─○─┼───┼─○─┼───│
   E  │───┼─○─┼───┼─●─┼───┼───┼─○─│
        2   3   4   5   6   7   8
            ◆       ◆       ◆
       └────box 5────┘
                   └────box 1────┘

This seam is even cleaner: all six shared notes sit on fret 5.

   e/5 = A      B/5 = E      G/5 = C
   D/5 = G      A/5 = D      E/5 = A

One fret, six notes, and two of them are roots. Compare with the box 1 → box 2 seam, which is split across frets 7 and 8 — seams are not all the same shape, and knowing which are single-fret is genuinely useful when you're moving fast.

Test: start at fret 2, play up through both boxes to fret 8, and back down. The fret-5 crossing should feel like one position, not two.

Drill 3.3 · The crossing unit​

Now isolate the join and treat it as its own two-second passage — the practice loop applied to four notes.

Take the G string at the box 1 → box 2 seam:

G/5 (C) → G/7 (D) → G/9 (E)          three notes, one string, one shift
             ↑
        the shared note — the shift happens here

Then the same idea across two strings:

D/5 (G) → D/7 (A) → A/7 (E) → A/10 (G)

Rules for this drill:

  • Four notes maximum. If you're playing more, you've stopped practising the seam and started practising a run.
  • Slow enough that the shift is deliberate. You should be able to feel the hand move, not discover that it moved.
  • Stop the moment it breaks and reset — the correct-reps-only principle from file 05.

Test: ten consecutive clean repetitions of one crossing unit at a slow tempo. Then raise the tempo by 5 BPM.

Drill 3.4 · Crossing with a slide​

The shift above is a lift-and-move. The alternative is to slide along the string into the new position, which is smoother, faster, and audible as a musical gesture rather than a mechanical one.

Take the same G-string crossing, but slide:

G/5 (C) ──slide──► G/7 (D)  ──►  G/9 (E)

Same notes; instead of lifting the finger, you keep pressure and move. Two practical notes:

  • Slide with the same finger you started with, or the shift doesn't save you anything.
  • The slide is a note, not just travel — it sounds, so it has to land in time.

Practise both versions of every crossing. The lift is more accurate; the slide is more musical. You want both available.

Test: play the same crossing twice, once lifted and once slid, and hear the difference clearly.

Why this page matters more than it looks​

Once you've done this, "five boxes" stops being five things to memorize. The boxes overlap, the overlaps are predictable, and what you're actually learning is one continuous pattern with convenient handholds — which is exactly what the full-neck view shows next.

Players who skip the seams end up with the opposite: five shapes, memorized separately, connected by guesswork and luck. That is box prison, and it's avoidable at this stage for very little work.

Takeaway​

  • The joins break phrases, not the shapes. Practise the joins as their own units.
  • Adjacent boxes overlap, and the shared notes are the seam — the crossing point is already built in.
  • Box 1 → Box 2: one shared note per string, split across frets 7 and 8.
  • Box 5 → Box 1: all six shared notes sit on fret 5 — a single-fret seam.
  • Crossing units: four notes maximum, slow, correct reps only.
  • Learn both crossings: the lift (accurate) and the slide (musical).
