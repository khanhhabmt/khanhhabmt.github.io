🎸 Electric Guitar Practice

This section is a self-teaching system for electric guitar, written for a specific starting point: someone who already plays acoustic.

That starting point matters more than it sounds. Coming from acoustic, you arrive with the two hardest things already solved — you can fret cleanly enough to make chords, and you can keep time. What you do not have is any of the machinery the electric guitar actually runs on. The first few weeks usually feel like a downgrade: everything is loud, messy, and buzzing, and it isn't obvious why.

The organizing question for the whole section:

The electric guitar is not a louder acoustic. So what is it, and what does that change about how you practice?

The two problems this section solves​

Problem 1 — the instrument is unfamiliar in ways nobody warns you about. Sustain, compression, gain, string noise, and an amplifier that is part of the instrument rather than an accessory. Acoustic technique transfers about 60%; the missing 40% is mostly noise control, and it's the reason a good acoustic player can sound bad on electric for months.

Problem 2 — you have no teacher, therefore no feedback loop. This is the real constraint on self-study, and it's structural rather than motivational. A teacher is not primarily a source of information — information is free and abundant. A teacher is an error-detection system. Without one you will practice your mistakes with perfect discipline. Most of this section is about building a replacement.

How this section is organized​

Two halves. The first is the instrument and the method; the second is one scale followed all the way from where the notes are to playing over changes.

The one idea to hold onto​

Practice is a search problem, and its cost is dominated by how fast you detect errors. Everything here — recording yourself, playing slowly, isolating one variable, using a clean tone — is one move: shorten the time between making a mistake and noticing it. Talent mostly looks like a fast feedback loop.
