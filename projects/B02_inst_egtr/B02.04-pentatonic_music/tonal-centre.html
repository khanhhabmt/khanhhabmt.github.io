The Tonal Centre

Same box. Same five notes. Same fingering. Two completely different sounds.

This page is the smallest possible version of the idea that runs the rest of the stage: a scale is a set of notes; a key is that set plus a gravity. Change nothing but the gravity and the music changes.

It comes before playing over changes on purpose. There, home moves four times per cycle. Here it moves once, and then stays put — the same skill at a speed you can actually hear.

1. The two homes​

File 09 established that A minor pentatonic and C major pentatonic are the same five notes rotated. That was a fact about sets. Here is the same fact as a sound.

Home = A. Minor: darker, more suspended, wants to fall.

Nothing moved except which three cells are filled in. Your left hand does not learn anything new on this page. Your ear does all the work.

2. Drill 3.1 · The same phrase, twice​

Record two backing loops: four minutes of Am, four minutes of C.

Play the identical phrase over both — same notes, same rhythm — but change only the note you end on: A over the first, C over the second.

Test: on playback, the two should sound like different pieces of music. If they sound the same, your ending notes are too short or landing off the beat.

That failure has a specific cause and a specific fix, so check it before assuming your ear is bad:

ending note too short      →  hold it for a full bar
ending note off the beat   →  land it on beat 1
ending followed by noise   →  leave two beats of silence after it

Gravity is created by duration, placement and silence. Three levers, all rhythmic, none of them about note choice — the same lesson as page 01, arriving from a different direction.

3. Drill 3.2 · Sing the root first​

Before playing over either loop, sing the root out loud — A for the minor track, C for the major one — and hold it until it feels obvious.

Then play. You will find your phrases bend toward whatever you sang.

This looks like a trick and it's actually the mechanism. A tonal centre is not information you apply; it's an expectation you carry. Installing it by singing is faster than installing it by thinking, and it survives being played fast, which thinking does not.

Test: have someone start one of the two loops without telling you which. Identify the home note within four bars, by ear.

4. Drill 3.3 · Switching mid-track​

Now one loop that alternates: eight bars of Am, eight bars of C, repeating.

Play continuously across the switch, changing only your landing note. Do not stop at the change, do not move your hand, do not alter your vocabulary. The only thing that changes is where phrases come to rest.

This is the actual skill, and it is much harder than either half separately.

Test: record four cycles. A listener who doesn't know the plan should be able to hear where the mood changed.

5. Why this generalizes: five homes, not two​

Nothing privileges A and C. Any of the five notes can be home, which gives five rotations of the same shape:

HomeWhat it's calledSound
AA minor pentatonicminor, the default rock sound
CC major pentatonicmajor, bright
DD pentatonic (suspended)modal, unresolved, folk-ish
EE pentatonic (phrygian-ish)dark, Spanish edge
GG pentatonicmixolydian-ish, hovering

A and C cover almost everything you'll actually play, which is why the other three are a footnote rather than a page. But knowing they exist changes what the box is: not "the A minor shape" but one set of five notes with five available gravities, of which you routinely use two.

This is the same move as the modes of the diatonic scale, described in Where the Machine Stops — one set, several centres.

Optional drill: loop a Dm vamp and play the same box, landing on D. Nothing about your hand changes; the sound is unmistakably different.

6. What this buys you​

Three concrete things, all of which show up immediately:

You stop needing a new scale per song. A track in C major and a track in A minor are the same box. That's not a shortcut — it's what "relative major/minor" actually means in the hands.

You can play over a track whose key you don't know. Find the note that sounds like home by trying endings, then build phrases toward it. This is how players who "just play by ear" do it, and it's a procedure, not a gift.

The next page becomes possible. Playing over changes is this skill applied four times faster, and if the slow version isn't reliable, the fast one has nothing to stand on.

Takeaway​

  • A scale is a set of notes; a key is that set plus a gravity. Same box, two sounds.
  • A home = minor, C home = major. Your hand learns nothing new here — only your ear.
  • Gravity comes from duration, placement and silence, not from note choice.
  • Sing the root before playing. A tonal centre is an expectation you carry, not information you apply.
  • Switching mid-track without moving your hand is the real drill, and it's harder than either key alone.
  • There are five possible homes, not two — the box is a set of notes with several available gravities. A and C cover the practical cases.
  • This is over changes at one-quarter speed. Get it reliable here first.
