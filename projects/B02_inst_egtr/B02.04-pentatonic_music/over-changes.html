Playing Over Changes

Every note of A minor pentatonic fits the key of A minor. So you loop Am – F – C – G, play the box over all of it, nothing sounds wrong — and nothing sounds like anything either. It wanders. The chords change and your solo doesn't appear to notice.

This page is about fixing that, and it is deliberately weighted toward what to actually do. The theory underneath is about four sentences long; the skill takes weeks. Those are not the same problem.

1. The reframe​

Three shifts, in order of how much they change your playing.

(a) The question isn't which notes are allowed. It's which note is home right now. All five notes stay available the whole time — nothing gets forbidden. What moves is gravity: which note feels like arrival and which feels like transit. Same furniture, different light.

(b) A target note is a rhythmic fact, not a theoretical one. A note becomes a target because it is long, or lands on beat 1, or is simply where you stopped. Play the identical five notes with different landing points and the chord changes appear. This is the single most practical idea on the page: you don't add notes to follow the changes, you redistribute emphasis.

(c) The listener hears arrivals, not runs. A run is the road; the target is the address. Nobody remembers the road.

And one thing worth noticing before you start: you already do this. Over an Am vamp you instinctively land on A. Playing over changes is that same instinct, updated four times per cycle instead of once.

2. What actually changes, note by note​

Here is the whole theory, as a table. Am F C G against A minor pentatonic (A C D E G):

ChordChord tonesAvailable in the scaleMissingTense note
AmA C EA, C, E — all three——
FF A CA, CF (the root)E
CC E GC, E, G — all three——
GG B DG, DB (the third)C

Three things fall out of it, and each one is directly usable.

The two missing notes are F and B — exactly the two notes the pentatonic dropped. That isn't a coincidence: the pentatonic is the diatonic scale minus its two semitone-clash notes (Pentatonic First, §1). So the scale is complete over the tonic pair (Am, C) and slightly short over the other two. Knowing where it runs out is more useful than pretending it doesn't.

The tense note is the pentatonic's stand-in for the note it's missing. Over F you have no F, and the nearest thing you own is E — a semitone below it. Over G you have no B, and you own C — a semitone above it. Hence the rule:

Pass through the tense note, don't park on it. It is fine in motion and bad as a landing.

Three of the four changes share a note; one doesn't.

Am → F     share A and C
F  → C     share C
C  → G     share G
G  → Am    share nothing

So G → Am is the one change you must move on, and it's the turnaround home. The other three you can survive by holding still — which is a technique, not a cop-out: hold the common tone and let the chord move underneath it. The note doesn't change; its meaning does.

3. The map, one chord at a time​

Same box 1, frets 5–8, four times. ● = a chord tone of that chord, safe to land on. ○ = in the scale, use in passing. ✕ = the tense note — pass through only.

Over Am — everything is a landing. This is why the vamp felt easy.

   e  │──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──·──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──·──┼─────│
   D  │──·──┼─────┼──●──┼─────│
   A  │──·──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

Over F — the Es become passing notes, and there is no ● at all: F is the one chord in the cycle whose root you don't own.

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │──✕──┼─────┼─────┼──·──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──·──┼─────│
   D  │──·──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │──·──┼─────┼──✕──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

Over C — everything is a landing again, but a different everything.

   e  │──·──┼─────┼─────┼──●──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   G  │──●──┼─────┼──·──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──·──┼─────│
   A  │──·──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──·──┼─────┼─────┼──●──│
         5     6     7     8

Over G — the thin one. Four landings out of twelve.

   e  │──·──┼─────┼─────┼──✕──│
   B  │──·──┼─────┼─────┼──●──│
   G  │──✕──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──●──┼─────┼──·──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──·──┼─────│
   E  │──·──┼─────┼─────┼──✕──│
         5     6     7     8

Do not try to memorize four grids. They're here so you can see the pattern breathe — dense, thin, dense, thin. What you memorize is section 4.

4. The ladder​

Seven steps. Each one is a complete exercise, and each assumes the one before. Backing: loop Am F C G, one bar each, around 70 BPM. Record yourself playing the four chords for four minutes if you don't have a track — you need a harmonic reference from outside yourself, for the reason in file 02.

Step 1 · Hear it, with no guitar​

Put the guitar down. Play the loop and sing or hum the root of each chord as it arrives: A – F – C – G. Then just tap on the chord change without singing.

This looks like a warm-up and is actually the bottleneck. The hard part of playing over changes is noticing the change, in time, while your hands are busy. If you can't do it with your hands empty, you certainly can't do it mid-phrase.

Test: four cycles where you land on the change every time, no drift.

Step 2 · One note per chord​

Now the guitar. One note per bar, on beat 1, held for the whole bar. Nothing else. Use these four:

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   G  │──C──┼─────┼──D──┼─────│
   D  │──G──┼─────┼──A──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8
Am  →  A   (D string, fret 7)      the root
F   →  C   (G string, fret 5)      the fifth of F
C   →  G   (D string, fret 5)      the fifth of C
G   →  D   (G string, fret 7)      the fifth of G

Four notes, two strings, two frets — a small rectangle. That is the entire exercise, and it is already music. Four held notes over four chords sounds intentional in a way that a fast box run does not. If that surprises you, it's the clearest possible evidence for reframe (b).

Test: eight cycles, every note landing exactly on beat 1, no scrambling.

Step 3 · Two notes per chord​

Same targets, but now approach each one: one note before it, on beat 4 of the previous bar, then the target on beat 1.

bar 3 ....................|  bar 4
              G/7 (D)     |  D/7 (A)
              beat 4      |  beat 1

The approach note can be anything in the scale — a step above or below the target is the easiest to hear. The point is that the target now has a lead-in, which is what makes an arrival sound deliberate rather than accidental.

Test: the approach note never makes you late for beat 1. If it does, use a closer approach note.

Step 4 · Land early​

Arrive on the "and" of beat 4 — a half-beat before the chord changes.

This is the step that suddenly sounds professional, and the reason is simple: an early arrival predicts the chord, a late one describes it. Arriving on beat 2 means you're commenting on a chord the listener already heard.

Test: alternate — one cycle landing on beat 1, one landing early. Hear the difference.

Step 5 · Thirds instead of fifths​

Swap the safe targets for the characteristic ones. The third is the note that tells you major from minor, so it carries far more information than the fifth:

Am  →  C    the minor third        G/5   or  E/8
F   →  A    the major third        D/7   or  E/5
C   →  E    the major third        A/7   or  B/5
G   →  D    the fifth              G/7   ← no third available

G keeps its fifth because the pentatonic has no B. Sit with that: it's why the G chord sounds vaguer than the others no matter what you do, and it's the first place you'll want a note from outside the scale. That's a later page, not this one.

Test: run the thirds set and the fifths set back to back. The thirds version should sound more committed.

Step 6 · Hold the common tone​

Take Am → F and play one note across both bars: A, held. The note doesn't move; the chord moves under it, and A converts from root to major third.

Do the same for F → C (hold C) and C → G (hold G). Then try it on G → Am and hear it fail — no shared note, so something has to move.

Test: name which change is the one that forces you to move, without checking.

Step 7 · Free, with the target still felt​

Now play whatever you want — but the target still has to arrive on time. Fill the bar however you like; the last note before each change is not free.

Constraint that keeps this honest: stay under six notes per bar. Space is what makes the targets audible, and the failure mode of this step is filling every gap until the arrivals disappear again.

Test: in section 5.

5. How to tell it's working​

The real test, and it's brutal:

Record your solo. Delete the backing track. Play the solo alone — can a listener tell where the chords changed?

If yes, you're playing over changes. If it's just a stream of pentatonic, you're playing in a key over a track that happens to be moving. Both are legitimate sounds; only one of them is this skill.

A note on gear, since it cuts against the usual advice: moderate gain is fine here. File 04 says learn it clean, because gain hides articulation problems — but this drill isn't measuring articulation. It's measuring note choice and timing, and sustain actually helps, because a held target rings long enough for you to hear whether it belongs. Practise the drills from the fretboard set clean; practise this one with the sound you'd actually use.

6. The four ways this goes wrong​

FailureWhat it sounds likeFix
Playing faster and hopingcontinuous notes, no shapeback to step 2 — one note per bar
Arriving latethe solo lags the band by a beatstep 4 — land early
Hunting for the targeta scramble before every changethe target must be hand memory, not a lookup; the root map is the prerequisite
Knowing the table, never loopingit works when you think about it and vanishes at tempothis is a hearing skill; it only comes from repetitions against a track

The third one is the reason this page comes after the fretboard set. If finding C in box 1 takes you a second of searching, you cannot spend that second on anything musical.

7. Takeaway​

  • The scale doesn't change; gravity does. No note becomes forbidden — one becomes home.
  • A target is rhythmic, not theoretical. Long, or on beat 1, or where you stopped. Same notes, redistributed emphasis.
  • Against Am F C G, the pentatonic covers Am and C completely, and is one note short on F (no root) and G (no third) — which are exactly the two notes the pentatonic dropped.
  • The tense note is the stand-in for the missing one: E over F, C over G. Pass through, don't park.
  • G → Am is the only change with no common tone — the one you must move on. On the other three you can hold still and let the chord recolour the note.
  • Start at one note per bar — four held notes is already music.
  • Land early, on the "and" of 4: early predicts the chord, late describes it.
  • The test: delete the backing track. Can anyone hear where the chords went?
