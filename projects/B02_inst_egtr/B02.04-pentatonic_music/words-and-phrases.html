Words and Phrases

Page 01 was about shaping a phrase. This page is about having something to shape: vocabulary.

The objection first, because most self-taught players carry it: isn't learning licks cheating? No. Nobody invents a language from scratch. You learn phrases, you use them, you modify them, and eventually the modifications are yours. A guitarist with no licks isn't original — they're mute.

A lick is a word. The skill isn't collecting words, it's conjugating them.

That distinction decides everything on this page. Five licks you can bend, stretch, truncate and re-rhythm will carry you much further than fifty you can only recite at one tempo in one place.

1. How to learn a lick properly​

Four steps, and step 4 is the one everybody skips.

  1. Get it by ear if you possibly can. Slower, and it trains the thing you actually need. Tab is a fallback, not a default — see the argument in Two Traditions about which index you're building.
  2. Play it slowly until it's clean, per the correct-reps rule in file 05.
  3. Learn where it sits — which box, which degrees of the scale. "The one on the top two strings" is not knowing where it sits.
  4. Move it. Different box, different key, different rhythm, different starting beat. A lick that only works in one position at one tempo is a party trick.

Step 4 is what converts a lick from a memorised object into vocabulary.

2. Five licks worth owning​

All in box 1, A minor pentatonic, frets 5–8. Numbers show the order of play.

Lick 1 · The descending four​

   e  │──2──┼─────┼─────┼──1──│
   B  │──4──┼─────┼─────┼──3──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

C (e/8) → A (e/5) → G (B/8) → E (B/5)

The default opening move in this box, and useful precisely because it's ordinary. Note that it ends on E, not on A — it's a question, in the sense of drill 1.4. Follow it with something that answers.

Lick 2 · The resolve​

   e  │──3──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │──1──┼─────┼─────┼──2──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

E (B/5) → G (B/8) → A (e/5)

Three notes, ending on the root, high. This is the answer to lick 1. The G → A move at the end — flat seventh resolving up to the root — is one of the most-used gestures in rock lead playing, and it's two notes.

Lick 3 · The money bend​

   e  │──2──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──1──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

G (B/8) ── bend a whole step ──► A          then e/5 (A) to check

Bend G on the B string up a whole tone until it is A, then play the real A on the high e string and compare. If they don't match, your bend is flat — which is the single most common intonation fault on electric guitar, and it is invisible to you without the reference note.

This is lick 2's ending, played as a bend instead of as two notes. Same resolution, completely different emotional weight. That substitution — play the arrival as a bend rather than as a fretted note — works everywhere.

Lick 4 · The repeating triplet​

   e  │──2──┼─────┼─────┼──3──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──1──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

G (B/8) → A (e/5) → C (e/8),  repeated as triplets

Three notes, cycled. This is the rule of three built into a lick: repetition creates tension by itself, with no new material. Play it four times and then break the pattern — the break is where the music is.

Lick 5 · Double stops​

Two notes at once, both struck together.

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│          C + E  (a third)
   B  │──1──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│          the C-major colour
   G  │──1──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8
   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│          G + C  (a fourth)
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│          the open, modal colour
   G  │──2──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──2──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

Double stops are the cheapest way to sound bigger. They also bridge lead and rhythm playing, which matters if you're the only guitarist.

3. Bends: where they actually live​

Bending is not decoration, it's the electric guitar's main expressive device (file 03). And the pentatonic tells you exactly where the whole-step bends belong. Take every note in box 1 and bend it up two semitones:

C → D    in scale          A → B     outside
D → E    in scale          E → F♯    outside
G → A    in scale

There's a rule underneath that:

C, D and G sit below a whole-tone step, so a whole-step bend lands on a scale note. A and E sit below a minor third, so the same bend lands outside.

Those two large gaps are the same ones you saw as 3 in the single-string gap pattern — 3, 2, 2, 3, 2. The scale's structure and the bend map are the same fact seen twice.

So: bend C, D or G freely. Bending A or E a whole step is a deliberate outside move (page 05), and bending them a minor third — three semitones, hard — lands back in the scale on C and G respectively.

Four bend types worth separating, easiest first:

TypeWhat it isWhere to use it
Blues curla quarter tone, deliberately between noteson C over a minor blues — pure attitude, no pitch commitment
Full benda full whole tone, in tunethe arrival note of a phrase
Pre-bendbend silently, strike, release downdramatic descents
Bend and releasebend up, then back, all soundingmid-phrase colour

Vibrato is separate and harder: it's a bend applied repeatedly, and an untidy vibrato will undo an otherwise good phrase. Practise it slow and wide before fast and narrow — a slow wide vibrato you control beats a fast one you don't.

4. Drill 2.1 · Conjugate one lick​

Take lick 1 and produce eight versions. Not eight licks — eight versions of the same one:

1  change the rhythm          (straight → dotted, or triplets)
2  start it on beat 2         instead of beat 1
3  play it backwards          E → G → A → C
4  truncate it                first two notes only, repeated
5  extend it                  add a fifth note
6  end it on A                make it an answer instead of a question
7  bend the last note         instead of fretting it
8  play it in box 2           frets 7–10, same shape logic

Two minutes each. By version 5 you'll be inventing rather than executing, which is the whole point of the exercise.

Test: play four bars using only versions of lick 1, and have it not sound repetitive.

5. Drill 2.2 · Speak in sentences​

Set the backing to Am, and play four bars built like language:

bar 1    lick 1                        a statement
bar 2    silence, or a held note        breathing
bar 3    lick 1, modified               restatement
bar 4    lick 2 or 3                    the answer, ending on A

Then swap the licks around and do it again. You are practising arrangement, not technique — the notes are already easy.

Test: record it. Can you hear four separate events, or one continuous stream? Four events is the pass.

6. The failure modes​

FailureTellFix
Lick stitchinglicks played back to back with no spacedrill 2.2 — bar 2 is silence
Collectingyou know twenty licks and use threestop learning new ones; run drill 2.1
One-position licksit falls apart outside frets 5–8step 4 of section 1 — move it
Flat bendsit sounds sour and you can't hear whylick 3, always with the reference note

Lick stitching is the big one, and it's the natural result of learning vocabulary before phrasing. That's exactly why page 01 came first.

Takeaway​

  • A lick is a word. Learning them isn't cheating; only reciting them is.
  • Four steps: by ear → slow and clean → know where it sits → move it. Step four is what makes it vocabulary.
  • Five licks worth owning: descending four (a question), the resolve (its answer), the money bend, the repeating triplet, double stops.
  • The G → A move — flat seventh up to the root — is two notes and does a lot of work.
  • Bend C, D, G and you land in the scale. A and E don't — they sit below the two minor-third gaps. Same fact as the 3, 2, 2, 3, 2 pattern.
  • Always check a bend against the real note. Flat bends are the most common fault and you cannot hear them unaccompanied.
  • Conjugate, don't collect: eight versions of one lick beats eight licks.
  • The failure to avoid is lick stitching — vocabulary with no sentences.
