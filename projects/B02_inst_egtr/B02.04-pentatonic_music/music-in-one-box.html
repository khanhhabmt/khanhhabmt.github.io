Music in One Box

Five notes, four frets, no new information. Everything on this page uses box 1 and nothing else.

That's the point. The gap between "running a scale" and "playing music" is not made of notes — you already have every note you need. It's made of rhythm, space, repetition and resolution, and none of those appear on a fretboard diagram, which is why a player can know the neck perfectly and still sound like homework.

A solo is not a sequence of correct notes. It's a sequence of arrivals, with distance between them.

1. The diagnosis first​

Record sixty seconds of yourself soloing over an Am vamp, then listen back with a pen. Count two things:

number of notes played
number of rests longer than one beat

If the second number is under five, that's the whole problem, and it's fixable this week. Almost every self-taught solo has the same shape: continuous eighth notes, no silence, no repeats, ending wherever the backing stopped.

The fixes below are ordered by how much they change the sound per unit of effort. The first one is the biggest and the least fun.

2. Drill 1.1 · One note, eight bars​

One note. A on the D string, fret 7. For eight bars.

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──1──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

Your only variables are when you play it, how long you hold it, and how loud. Make eight bars that are worth listening to.

This is uncomfortable and it's the most valuable drill in this section. With pitch removed, the only thing left is rhythm — so if the result is boring, you have located your actual weakness, and it was never note choice.

Test: record it. Can you hear a phrase — something with a beginning and an end — or just a note being repeated?

3. Drill 1.2 · Three notes, one motif​

Add two notes. Three total:

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   G  │──2──┼─────┼──3──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──1──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

1 = A (D/7)      2 = C (G/5)      3 = D (G/7)

Build one motif — a short shape, two to four notes, that you can sing. Then play only that motif for two minutes, varying rhythm and placement but not the notes.

A motif is the unit of musical memory. Solos people remember are made of a small number of motifs, restated; solos people don't remember are made of many notes, each used once.

Test: can you sing your motif back an hour later? If not, it was a run, not a motif.

4. Drill 1.3 · The rule of three​

Take the motif from 1.2 and structure four bars like this:

bar 1    motif
bar 2    motif again, identical
bar 3    motif again, identical
bar 4    motif CHANGED — different ending, or extended, or resolved

Repetition is what makes a listener recognize something; the change on the fourth is what makes it go somewhere. This one structure underlies an enormous amount of blues and rock lead playing, and you can execute it today with three notes.

The instinct to fight here is variety anxiety — the feeling that repeating yourself is lazy. It isn't. Unrepeated material is not variety, it's noise.

Test: play the four bars for someone who doesn't play guitar and ask whether the last bar sounded like an ending.

5. Drill 1.4 · Question and answer​

Now use all five notes, but structure them in pairs of phrases:

bars 1–2    QUESTION    end on a note that is NOT A     (C, D, E or G)
bars 3–4    ANSWER      end on A

This is where the note names from Stage 1 start paying rent. A sounds finished; the other four sound unfinished, and that is a tool, not a defect. Ending a phrase on D and leaving space creates an expectation you can then satisfy.

Two rules that make it work:

  • The ending note must be long. A target note that lasts a semiquaver isn't a target, it's a passing note. Duration is what makes the ear treat a note as an arrival.
  • Leave the space. The question needs air after it, or the answer doesn't read as an answer.

Test: record four such pairs. On playback, you should be able to hear the question marks.

6. Drill 1.5 · Sing it first​

Turn on the backing, don't play, and sing a phrase. Then find it in the box and play it.

You will be slow at this and you will discover that what you sing is much simpler than what your hands do unsupervised. That gap is the entire lesson: your hands have habits, your voice doesn't. What you sing is what you actually mean.

This is also the only drill here that improves your ear rather than your vocabulary, which makes it the one with the longest payoff.

Test: sing four bars, then play them back accurately. If you can't find them on the neck, that's a Stage 1 gap, not a Stage 2 one.

7. Drill 1.6 · Half the notes​

Record a solo the way you'd naturally play it. Then play the same thing again with half as many notes — same phrases, same shape, notes removed.

Almost always the second version is better. Do it a third time at half again if you can stand it.

What you are training is a policy, not a technique: when in doubt, remove. The self-taught default is to add, because adding feels like progress and looks like effort.

Test: put the two recordings side by side and pick the better one honestly.

8. Running this page​

3 min   1.1  one note, rhythm only
3 min   1.2  build a motif
3 min   1.3  rule of three
3 min   1.4  question and answer
2 min   1.5  sing then play

Drill 1.6 isn't a daily drill — it's a monthly audit. Run it whenever a recording sounds cluttered.

Takeaway​

  • You already have every note you need. The missing skills are rhythm, space, repetition and resolution.
  • One note for eight bars removes pitch and exposes rhythm — the most uncomfortable and most useful drill here.
  • Build motifs, not runs. A motif is the unit of musical memory: singable, short, restated.
  • Rule of three: state it, state it, state it, change it.
  • A sounds finished and the other four don't — that's a tool. Question phrase, answer phrase.
  • Target notes must be long. A short note is never heard as an arrival.
  • Sing it first: your voice has no bad habits, your hands do.
  • Default policy: when in doubt, remove.
