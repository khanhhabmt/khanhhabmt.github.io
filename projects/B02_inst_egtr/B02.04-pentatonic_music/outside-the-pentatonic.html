Outside the Pentatonic

The pentatonic is safe because of what it leaves out. Eventually the same omissions become the limitation — most visibly on the G chord in page 04, which stays vague no matter what you do, because the scale has no B to give it.

This page adds three notes, in the order that gives the most sound for the least risk. It is last for a reason:

New notes fix nothing that phrasing was failing to do. If your solos sound aimless with five notes, they will sound aimless with eight.

1. The governing rule​

One rule covers every note on this page:

An outside note is a passing note until you've earned it. What makes a note sound wrong is not playing it — it's landing on it.

Duration is the whole mechanism. E♭ played on the way from D to E is the most idiomatic sound in blues. The same E♭ held for a bar over Am sounds like a mistake, because you told the listener it was an arrival.

So for everything below: short, in motion, between two scale notes. You can relax that later, per note, once you can hear what each one does.

2. Note one · The blue note (E♭)​

The flat fifth. Sits in the gap between D and E — one of the two minor-third gaps from the single-string pattern.

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼──✚──┼──○──│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼──✚──┼──○──┼─────┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
         5     6     7     8     9

Two positions in this window: A/6 and G/8. That's it — the blue note is rare on the neck, which is part of why it's effective.

Adding it makes the blues scale: A C D E♭ E G. Six notes.

The drill: play D → E♭ → E and E → E♭ → D on the A string (A/5 → A/6 → A/7), over and over, always moving. Then use it inside lick 1 from page 02 as a passing note.

Why it's first: highest payoff, lowest risk, and it is unambiguous — nobody hears a passing E♭ as a wrong note. It sounds like a decision.

Test: eight bars where every E♭ is short and in motion. If one rings, you hear it immediately.

3. Note two · The major third (C♯)​

The one that changes the colour rather than adding spice.

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼──✚──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   G  │──○──┼──✚──┼──○──┼─────┼──○──│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼──✚──│
         5     6     7     8     9

Playing C♯ against a scale that also contains C is the major/minor blur — Chuck Berry, Hendrix, Stevie Ray Vaughan, most of blues-rock. It works because a dominant chord like A7 contains C♯ while the vocabulary around it stays minor.

Two honest conditions, because this one can sound wrong:

  • It needs the harmony to support it. Over a straight Am, C♯ fights the chord's minor third. Over A7 or a blues in A, it's the point.
  • The classic move is C → C♯ in motion, or a bend from C up toward C♯ — a half-step bend, not the whole-step bends from page 02.

The drill: over a 12-bar blues in A, play box 1 as usual but slide C → C♯ on the G string (G/5 → G/6) once per phrase. One per phrase, not one per bar.

Test: play the same phrase with and without it over an A7 vamp. If you can't hear the difference, the harmony isn't dominant enough — check the backing.

4. Note three · The B​

The one page 04 promised.

   e  │──○──┼─────┼──✚──┼──○──┼─────│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼──✚──│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼──✚──┼──○──┼─────│
         5     6     7     8     9

B is the third of the G chord — the note whose absence made G sound noncommittal in the progression Am F C G. Add it and G snaps into focus.

It has two other jobs worth knowing:

  • Over Am it's the ninth — a soft, floating colour, and safe to hold, unlike the two notes above.
  • It is where the A → B whole-step bend lands. Per page 02, bending A a whole tone goes outside the pentatonic — this is where it goes, and now it's a note you meant.

The drill: the target-note exercise from page 04, but on the G bar, target B (E/7, D/9 or e/7) instead of D. Play the cycle both ways and hear which one sounds like it knows what the chord is.

Test: over Am F C G, land on B for the G bar, four cycles, in time.

5. What you've actually built​

Add the two notes the pentatonic dropped — F and B — and you have the whole thing back:

   e  │──○──┼─────┼──✚──┼──○──┼─────│
   B  │──○──┼──✚──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────┼──✚──│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼──✚──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼──✚──┼──○──┼─────│
         5     6     7     8     9

○ = A minor pentatonic          ✚ = F and B

That is A natural minor — the full seven-note scale, which is also C major. Which means the claim made back in Pentatonic First was literal, not motivational:

The pentatonic is a skeleton, not a detour. Everything you learned is kept.

You didn't learn a beginner's scale and then replace it. You learned five of the seven notes, in every position, with their roots — and the remaining two slot into gaps you already know the shape of. The F and B are the two semitone-clash notes from Pentatonic First §1, which is exactly why they were the safe ones to postpone.

6. The order to add them, and the trap​

1   E♭   blue note      passing only        biggest effect, no risk
2   C♯   major third    needs A7 or blues   changes the colour
3   B    the ninth      safe to hold        fixes the G chord

Then F, and you're playing the full scale — at which point the questions change from which notes to which chord, and that's a different subject again.

The trap is treating this page as permission to use everything. Three added notes make the scale 60% bigger and make it 300% easier to sound unfocused. The players who use outside notes well use very few of them, very deliberately, and the reason they land is the space around them.

If a recording sounds worse after this page, go back to page 01 and halve the notes. That fix works here too.

Takeaway​

  • An outside note is a passing note until you've earned it. What makes a note sound wrong is landing on it, not playing it.
  • E♭ (blue note) first: two positions in the box, biggest effect, no risk. Always in motion, D → E♭ → E.
  • C♯ (major third) second: the major/minor blur. Needs A7 or a blues to support it; the move is C → C♯, or a half-step bend.
  • B third: the third of the G chord, the ninth over Am, and where the A whole-step bend lands. Safe to hold.
  • Add F and B and you have A natural minor — the pentatonic really was a skeleton, and nothing you learned gets replaced.
  • The trap: three extra notes make it far easier to sound unfocused. Use few, deliberately, with space around them.

That completes the pentatonic curriculum. From here the useful next subject isn't more scale — it's chords: what the backing is doing, and why the notes you're choosing work against it.
