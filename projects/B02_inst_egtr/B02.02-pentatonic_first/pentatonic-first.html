Pentatonic First

The pentatonic scale is where almost every self-teaching guitarist starts, and where a large number of them get stuck for years. Both facts have the same cause: the scale is easy to play and easy to learn badly.

This file is the applied version of the method in the Electric Guitar pages — a concrete route through the pentatonic that avoids the two standard traps (memorizing five shapes you can't use, and getting locked inside one box).

The organizing idea, which halves the work:

There are five shapes, not ten. Major and minor pentatonic are the same five notes and the same five shapes. The only difference is which note you treat as home.

1. Why the pentatonic is worth this much attention​

There's a structural reason it's forgiving, and it's measurable. Counting how often each interval class appears in each scale:

diatonic (7 notes)     [2, 5, 4, 3, 6, 1]
pentatonic (5 notes)   [0, 3, 2, 1, 4, 0]
                        ↑                ↑
                     no semitones    no tritone

The pentatonic is the scale with no semitones and no tritone — precisely the two most dissonant interval classes. The diatonic scale contains pitches that clash against the tonic chord if you land on them; the pentatonic is what's left after those are removed. So "you can't play a wrong note" isn't encouragement, it's a consequence of the interval content. The full derivation is in Why Seven Carries Tonality.

Stated precisely, because the loose version causes trouble later:

The pentatonic has no notes that are wrong for the key. It still has notes that are tense against a particular chord.

The dissonance was removed relative to the tonic, not relative to every chord you might play over. In A minor pentatonic over a G chord, C sits a semitone above the chord's third B — the classic avoid note over a major triad. Over an F chord, E rubs against the root a semitone below (usually welcome, since it just reads as Fmaj7, but it is a rub).

That gap is the whole difference between playing in a key and playing over changes, and it's why target notes shift chord by chord once you get there. For now the working version holds: nothing you land on sounds wrong against the tonic, so you can spend your attention on time and phrasing instead of note choice.

Four reasons it's specifically good on guitar:

  • Best payoff per note learned. Five notes and five shapes cover the majority of rock, blues and pop lead vocabulary.
  • The fretboard rewards shapes. Movable shapes are the guitar's structural advantage — see Shapes vs Names — and the pentatonic is the first shape system worth internalizing.
  • The gaps are where bends live. The minor-third gaps in the scale are exactly where blues bending vocabulary sits. Given that bends are a priority for anyone arriving from acoustic (file 03), this is where you train them.
  • It's a skeleton, not a detour. The major scale is the pentatonic plus two notes. Everything you learn here is kept, not replaced.

2. Major vs minor: the same five notes​

This is the question that actually trips people up, and settling it now removes half the work in section 4.

Major and minor pentatonic are the same five notes, rotated — the relative major/minor relationship, identical in kind to the modes described in Where the Machine Stops.

C major pentatonic:  C  D  E  G  A
A minor pentatonic:  A  C  D  E  G
                     └── same set {A, C, D, E, G} ──┘

Measured in semitones from their own root:

major pentatonic    1  2  3  5  6      →  0, 2, 4, 7,  9
minor pentatonic    1 ♭3  4  5 ♭7      →  0, 3, 5, 7, 10

On the guitar this means the shapes are literally identical. Section 4 shows this directly: the same diagram, with different notes marked as home.

The trap is that this works too well. You can play the A-minor box over a C major backing track and it sounds fine — because the notes are the same — without having any idea what you're doing. That's the difference between playing safe notes and playing music: same pitches, but different notes want to resolve, different notes belong on strong beats, and the bend targets are different.

Aside — accidentals. Whether a pentatonic contains sharps or flats is a property of the key, not of the scale type — exactly as C major has none while D major has F♯. C D E G A has no accidentals, D E F♯ A B has one, and F♯ G♯ A♯ C♯ D♯ has five. That last one is worth knowing as a landmark: the five black keys on a piano are an F♯ major pentatonic scale.

3. How to read these diagrams​

●  root note (home)          ──●──   a fret space with a scale note in it
○  other note in the scale   ─────   an empty fret space
                             ┼       a fret wire

Strings run left-to-right as they do on the neck; high E is the top line, low E the bottom, matching tab. Fret numbers are underneath.

4. The five shapes​

Worked in A minor pentatonic (A C D E G) — the most common scale shape on the guitar — which is simultaneously C major pentatonic.

Box 1 · frets 5–8​

The famous one. If you learn one shape, this is it.

   e  │──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──●──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
         5     6     7     8

Same box with the notes named — worth memorizing in this form, not just as a shape:

   e  │──A──┼─────┼─────┼──C──│
   B  │──E──┼─────┼─────┼──G──│
   G  │──C──┼─────┼──D──┼─────│
   D  │──G──┼─────┼──A──┼─────│
   A  │──D──┼─────┼──E──┼─────│
   E  │──A──┼─────┼─────┼──C──│
         5     6     7     8

And here is section 2 made visible. Identical fingering, but with C lit up as home instead of A — the same box is now C major pentatonic:

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──●──│
   B  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   G  │──●──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──●──│
         5     6     7     8

Not one finger moves. Only the target changes.

Box 2 · frets 7–10​

   e  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──●──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   A  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   E  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──│
         7     8     9    10

Box 3 · frets 9–13​

The awkward one — note the three-fret gaps on the G and B strings. Everyone finds this shape uncomfortable; it isn't you.

   e  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   B  │─────┼──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   D  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──●──┼─────│
   E  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
         9    10    11    12    13

Box 4 · frets 12–15​

   e  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   B  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──│
   G  │──○──┼─────┼──●──┼─────│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼──○──┼─────│
   A  │──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   E  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
        12    13    14    15

Box 5 · frets 14–17 (also frets 2–5)​

   e  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──●──│
   B  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──│
   G  │──●──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   D  │──○──┼─────┼─────┼──○──│
   A  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──○──│
   E  │─────┼──○──┼─────┼──●──│
        14    15    16    17

The neck repeats every twelve frets, so this same shape also sits at frets 2–5, which is where you'll actually play it. Box 5 at fret 2 leads straight into box 1 at fret 5.

A warning about position numbers​

Box numbering is not standardized. Different books, apps and teachers number these differently, and some use wider five-fret spans instead of two-notes-per- string. If the material you're using labels them differently, neither source is wrong.

The fix is to stop relying on the number: anchor on where the root is. "The box with roots on the 6th and 1st strings" is unambiguous; "position 1" is not.

5. All twelve keys, free​

Because the fret axis is uniform, these shapes transpose by sliding — the structural property derived in The Neck as a Coordinate System. Learn the five shapes once and you have all twelve keys.

Take box 1 and read its 6th-string root:

Root fret (6th string)Minor pentatonic= Major pentatonic
0 / 12E minorG major
1 / 13F minorA♭ major
2 / 14F♯ minorA major
3 / 15G minorB♭ major
4 / 16G♯ minorB major
5 / 17A minorC major
6B♭ minorD♭ major
7B minorD major
8C minorE♭ major
9C♯ minorE major
10D minorF major
11E♭ minorG♭ major

Two rows to note. Fret 5 is the worked example above. Fret 2 is A major pentatonic — so if you're learning A major pentatonic, it's box 1 at fret 2 and the identical five shapes running up from there.

The rule in one line: minor root + 3 semitones = relative major root.

6. The learning path​

The common route — learn box 1, then 2, then 3, 4, 5, in order, as shapes — reliably produces a player who can run boxes and can't make music. The order below fixes that by delaying shapes and prioritizing roots and phrasing.

Stage 1 — One box, with its roots. Box 1 in A minor. Learn the shape and the three root locations (E6/5, D4/7, e1/5) at the same time. Roots are what turn a shape into music.

Test: play the box and stop on a root, on command, from anywhere in it.

Stage 2 — Make music with only that. Backing track in A minor, stay in the box, and work on phrasing: bends, vibrato, leaving space, ending phrases on strong notes. Aim to sound good with five notes in one position.

Test: record 30 seconds. Does it sound like music or like a scale? This is the recording habit doing its job.

Spend much longer here than feels reasonable. This is the stage that separates players who sound musical from players who sound like exercises, and it's the one everyone rushes.

Stage 3 — Go horizontal before you go wide. Take two strings only (the G and B are ideal) and play the scale along the whole neck. This breaks box-thinking before it hardens, and it's the single most effective inoculation against box prison.

Test: play from fret 2 to fret 14 and back on two strings, in time, without looking for a box.

Stage 4 — Add the neighbours, and drill the seams. Add box 2 and box 5 — the boxes adjacent to box 1 — and practise the transitions as isolated units, not the boxes. Phrases break at the joins, not in the middle. Same "practise the seams" principle as file 07.

Test: start a phrase in box 1 and finish it in box 2 without a gap.

Stage 5 — The rest of the neck, root-first. Boxes 3 and 4. For each, learn where the roots are before the shape.

Test: name a key, and find its root in all five boxes in under a minute.

Stage 6 — Switch the tonal centre on. Play the same shapes over an A minor backing, then over a C major backing. Listen for which notes now feel like home. This is where section 2 stops being a fact and becomes a skill.

Test: over a C major track, land on C at the ends of phrases without hunting for it.

Stage 7 — Extend. Add the ♭5 to get the blues scale; later add the two missing notes to get the full diatonic. Both are additions to what you already own.

7. Three drills worth doing​

Two-string runs (breaks box prison). As in stage 3. Pick a string pair, play the scale the full length of the neck. Do this on all five pairs eventually.

Seam repetitions (fixes the joins). Isolate the four-note crossing between two adjacent boxes and run it with the practice loop — slow, clean, tempo ladder.

Bend targets (fixes pitch). The two classic bends both live in box 1:

G string, fret 7  (D, the 4th)   ── whole step ──►  E   (the 5th)
B string, fret 8  (G, the ♭7)    ── whole step ──►  A   (the root)

Use the target-note test from file 03: fret the target first so your ear holds it, then bend up and compare. Record it — bends that feel correct commonly land 20–30 cents flat.

8. How this goes wrong​

FailureTellFix
Box prisoneverything you play sits in frets 5–8stage 3, two-string runs
Shapes without rootsyou can run the box but can't stop on a rootstage 1, redone
Scale-runningyour playing sounds like ascending and descendingstage 2, phrasing over a track
Shape without soundyou play the right notes and don't know what they arethe named-note diagram
Box collectingyou know five shapes and can't solostop adding shapes; go back to stage 2

The last one is the most common outcome of the standard route, and the reason this file orders things the way it does. Five shapes is not the goal. One shape you can make music with is worth more than five you can only recite.

9. Takeaway​

  • The pentatonic has no semitones and no tritone — that's why there are no wrong notes, not encouragement.
  • Major and minor pentatonic are the same five notes and the same five shapes. Only the root changes — so you learn five shapes, not ten. (Whether a key has accidentals is a property of the key, not the scale.)
  • Five boxes in A minor / C major: 5–8, 7–10, 9–13, 12–15, 14–17 (= 2–5). Box 3 is genuinely awkward; that's the shape, not you.
  • Box numbering isn't standardized — anchor on root location, not on the number.
  • Shapes slide, so five shapes = twelve keys. Minor root + 3 semitones = relative major. A major pentatonic is box 1 at fret 2.
  • Path: one box with its roots → make music in it → go horizontal → drill the seams → the rest → switch tonal centre → extend. Stage 2 deserves far more time than it gets.
  • "No wrong notes" is true against the tonic, not against every chord — which is why target notes move once you play over changes.
  • The failure to avoid is collecting shapes you can't use.
