Two Traditions

Sooner or later a self-teaching player finds a book like William Leavitt's A Modern Method for Guitar (Berklee Press) and asks a fair question: this is the guitar curriculum of a serious music school, and it contains almost none of what I've been practising. So which one is wrong?

Neither. They are two traditions with different goals, producing different players — and each is close to silent about what the other does best.

Knowing which tradition you're in, and what it is not going to give you, is worth more than picking a side.

1. What A Modern Method actually is​

Leavitt built and ran Berklee's guitar department, and the book is that curriculum written down. Its method rests on a handful of decisions, all consistent with each other:

Reading is the spine. Standard notation throughout, no tab. The endpoint is concrete and testable: hand the player an unfamiliar chart and they perform it. Everything else in the book serves that goal.

The unit of organization is the position, not the shape. A position is the four-fret span defined by where the index finger sits. Learning "position V" means learning every note available there — all twelve pitch classes, in any key, plus the arpeggios running through it. Not a five-note subset.

Scales are reading and technique material, not improvising vocabulary. You run major scales in each position to bind eye, hand and note name together, not to stock a solo with phrases.

It assumes a teacher. A large share of the material is duets — student on one part, instructor on the other. That supplies pulse, pitch reference, and error detection from outside the player, which is exactly the machinery file 02 says a self-teacher is missing.

Harmony runs in parallel. Movable chord forms and voice leading from early on, building toward chord melody.

What that produces is a guitarist who reads, who knows note names everywhere on the neck, and who can function in an ensemble or a session. A specific and valuable animal — and not the same animal as a blues-rock improviser.

2. Why there are no pentatonic boxes in it​

This is not an oversight. In a position system, the pentatonic box is not a concept. It's an incidental subset of position V. There's no reason to name it, because you already know every note in that span — twelve, not five.

The two systems index the same fretboard in incompatible ways:

Position systemBox system
Lineageconservatory, jazz, session workblues → rock; oral, magazines, video
Frame of referenceabsolute note namesrelative shapes
Unita position: 12 pitch classes, 4 fretsa box: 5 notes, all six strings
Changing keyread the accidentals, hand stays putslide the shape
Improvising taught viachord-scale theoryshape + ear + licks
Notationstandard notationdiagrams and tab
Producesa readera soloist

There's a structural reason improvisation is thin in it too: A Modern Method is the reading-and-technique spine of a curriculum, not the whole curriculum. In the Berklee stream, improvising is a separate subject taught through chord-scale theory — which doesn't need pentatonic boxes either, because a player who knows every note in a position chooses notes from the chord rather than from a shape.

3. This is the shapes/names split, in book form​

The distinction isn't new to this section. It is exactly the one set out in Shapes vs Names: the fretboard supports two indexes over the same pitch space, and any method has to choose one to build on.

Leavitt / positions      →   the NAME index     absolute, positional, notation-driven
Pentatonic boxes         →   the SHAPE index    relative, movable, pattern-driven

Leavitt is, in effect, the pianist model imposed on the guitar — deliberately, because reading and ensemble work require it. See What Each Model Hides for why neither index is complete on its own.

Which means the complaint "that book doesn't teach any of this" has a symmetric form that's just as true, and more useful to hear.

4. What each one leaves out​

What the box tradition leaves out:

  • Reading. Most rock guitarists cannot read music, and this is why.
  • Rhythmic precision on paper — reading a written rhythm accurately is a separate skill from playing in time.
  • Note names away from the shape. The shape works without them, so they never get built. This is the failure mode Pentatonic First calls shape without sound.
  • Harmony. Boxes say nothing about which chord is under you.
  • Keys with accidentals. Shapes slide, so you can go years without meeting one consciously.

What Leavitt leaves out — and for an electric player the list is long:

  • The electric guitar as an electric instrument. No gain, no tone, no noise control, no amplifier. Leavitt's world is the archtop plectrum tradition. This is the missing forty percent identified in file 03.
  • Expression vocabulary. Bends, vibrato, slides-as-gesture — barely present, though they carry most of the meaning in rock and blues lead playing.
  • Groove. Rhythm-guitar feel is not what the book is measuring.
  • Early reward. It's a long time before you play something a listener recognizes as music.
  • Survivability alone. No teacher, no duet partner, no correction — the attrition rate for self-teachers working through it is high, and that is a property of the format, not of your discipline.

5. What to do about it​

Not "pick one." But also not "run both starting now" — adding a reading practice while you're still building the fretboard map splits attention across two indexes at once, which is the slowest possible way to acquire either.

The rule: one index at a time, until it's automatic.

now        →   the shape index      pentatonic drills, this section
trigger    →   drill 4.6 passes     any scale note, every instance, under 30 s
then       →   add the name index   Leavitt vol. 1, ~10 min/day, reading only

Three reasons for that order:

  1. The shape index pays out fastest in playable music. For a self-teacher the binding constraint is whether you keep going at all (file 06), and early payoff is what buys that.
  2. The drills already plant the name index. Naming notes aloud (drill 1.4) and the root map (drill 2.4) exist precisely so you don't build a habit you later have to break. You're not choosing shapes instead of names, only first.
  3. Leavitt's payoff is slow and needs consistency. Better attached to a routine that already exists than used to start one.

When you do add it, use it for what it's for — reading, ten minutes, as a side channel, not as your main practice. It fills the exact hole this section leaves.

Caveat, stated plainly. This order assumes your goal is playing rock or blues lead. If it turns out to be session work, teaching, or jazz, the order is wrong and you should be reading from day one — in that world, a player who can't read is unemployable regardless of how well they solo.

Takeaway​

  • A Modern Method is reading-first, position-based, teacher-assumed. Its goal is a guitarist who can perform an unfamiliar chart.
  • It has no pentatonic boxes because in a position system the box isn't a concept — you learn all twelve notes in the span, not five.
  • The two traditions are the name index and the shape index over the same neck. Leavitt is the pianist model applied to guitar.
  • Boxes leave out reading, note names, harmony. Leavitt leaves out everything electric — gain, tone, noise, bends, vibrato, groove — plus early reward.
  • One index at a time. Shapes now; add reading once drill 4.6 passes.
  • If the goal is session work or jazz, invert that order.
