Other Generators, Other Systems

The argument so far has one load-bearing assumption, made back in file 01: the generator is the fifth, because the third harmonic is the first non-octave partial of a vibrating string or pipe.

That premise is not universal. Some instruments don't have a harmonic series in that sense at all — and where they dominate, the whole derivation comes apart and different systems appear. This file is the control group for the previous four.

1. The premise, stated so it can be attacked​

IF   your instruments are strings and pipes
THEN their partials are near-integer multiples of the fundamental
THEN the 3rd partial (the fifth) is the salient non-octave interval
THEN stacking fifths is the natural generator
THEN you land on 5, 7, 12

Every arrow is sound. But the first line is a fact about instrument-building, not about music — and it fails for an entire family of instruments.

2. Gamelan: when the overtones aren't harmonic​

Javanese and Balinese gamelan is built mainly on metallophones and tuned gongs — struck bars and plates, not strings. And struck bars are inharmonic: their partials are not near-integer multiples of the fundamental. (The same physics that makes a piano's stiff strings drift sharp, described in Piano Inharmonicity, but far more extreme — for a bar, the partials aren't approximately harmonic at all.)

The consequence is fundamental: if there is no strong 3rd harmonic, the 3:2 fifth has no special claim. Consonance for these instruments is governed by how their actual inharmonic partials interact, which is a different problem with different answers.

What gamelan uses instead:

  • Slendro — five notes per octave, roughly (but not exactly) even.
  • Pelog — seven notes per octave, distinctly uneven, from which subsets of five are typically used.

Note that five and seven still appear — scale sizes are constrained by memory and melodic practicality regardless of generator. But the tuning of those notes owes nothing to stacked fifths.

Two features are worth knowing because they're so alien to the Western frame:

  • There is no standard tuning. Each gamelan is forged as a matched set with its own tuning, and two gamelans are generally not interchangeable — you cannot swap instruments between ensembles. Tuning is a property of the ensemble, not of the culture. The idea of a universal reference pitch like A = 440 simply has no analogue.
  • Beating is deliberate. Paired instruments are intentionally tuned slightly apart so that their interference produces a shimmering pulse (ombak, "wave"). Western tuning treats beats as the error signal to eliminate — the beatless fifth is the target in The Comma & Temperament. Gamelan treats them as the desired timbre.

If you want one example proving that Western pitch organization is a branch rather than a discovery, this is it.

3. Traditions that kept the fifth but divided differently​

Other systems accept harmonic instruments and the fifth, then make different choices about how finely to subdivide:

TraditionSystemNotes
Turkish (makam)octave in 53 commasThe Arel-Ezgi-Uzdilek theory divides the octave into 53 Holdrian commas — precisely the 53 that appears as a top-tier convergent in file 02 and again in file 06. Practice draws a smaller working set from it.
Arabic (maqam)notated in 24 quarter-tonesThe 24 is largely a notational convenience. Performed intervals — especially the "neutral" seconds and thirds — are not literally quarter-tones and vary by maqam, region and performer.
Indian (classical)22 shrutiMicrotonal positions derived from just ratios, from which 12 swara positions are selected in practice. The exact ratios are genuinely disputed among theorists; there is no single authoritative table.
Thai7 near-equal stepsA heptatonic system whose steps are approximately equal — unlike the diatonic's uneven 2 2 1 2 2 2 1.

A caution worth stating plainly: these one-line summaries compress living traditions with large internal disagreements, and several of the numerical frameworks above (24, 22, 53) are theoretical models used to describe practice after the fact, not rules performers follow. Where sources genuinely disagree — the shruti ratios especially — the honest position is that the disagreement is real, not that one table is correct.

4. What survives the comparison, and what doesn't​

Comparing across all of these, the claims from files 01–04 sort into two piles.

Holds up well:

  • Octave equivalence. Essentially universal.
  • Scale sizes of 5 and 7. These dominate worldwide, including in gamelan, where the fifth-stacking argument does not apply. That strongly suggests a second, independent constraint — how many pitches a melody can use and stay memorable — reinforcing the same answer for a different reason.

Culturally specific:

  • The fifth as generator. Requires harmonic instruments. Fails for gamelan.
  • Closing the set at twelve. Many traditions used 5 and 7 for centuries with no need to close under transposition. Twelve is the answer to a question — "play the same scale in every key" — that not every musical culture asked.
  • Even, fixed, standardized tuning. A Western industrial-era preoccupation. Gamelan explicitly rejects it.

That middle point is the sharpest one. Twelve isn't the natural endpoint of music; it's the natural endpoint of wanting free transposition on fixed-pitch instruments. Change the goal and the number changes.

5. Takeaway​

  • The fifth-as-generator argument depends on harmonic partials — true for strings and pipes, false for struck bars and gongs.
  • Gamelan (metallophones, inharmonic) uses slendro (~5) and pelog (7) with no fifth-stacking rationale, no standardized tuning between ensembles, and deliberate beating (ombak) where Western tuning eliminates it.
  • Makam (53 commas), maqam (24 notated), shruti (22), Thai (7 near-equal) keep harmonic instruments but subdivide differently — and several of these frameworks are descriptive models, with real scholarly disagreement.
  • Sizes 5 and 7 survive the comparison and look genuinely cross-cultural. Twelve does not — it's the answer to a specifically Western goal, free transposition on fixed-pitch instruments.
