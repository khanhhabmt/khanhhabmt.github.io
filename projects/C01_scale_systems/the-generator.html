One Interval, One Rule

Before you can ask "why seven notes," you need a machine that produces notes at all. This file builds the smallest one that works: one interval to stack, one rule for folding the results back into an octave. Everything in this section is that machine run at different settings.

1. The octave is the only thing you get for free​

Double a frequency and humans everywhere call the result "the same note, higher." This is about as close to a universal as music has — it shows up in every documented tradition, and it's why pitch systems repeat rather than climb forever.

So the octave 2:1 isn't a choice; it's the container. The real question is how to divide the space inside it. And the octave alone can't help you: stacking octaves only ever gives you C, C, C, C. You need a second interval — a generator — that doesn't land on octaves.

2. Why the generator is the fifth​

The generator isn't arbitrary either. Any vibrating string or air column produces a harmonic series — integer multiples of its fundamental — and those multiples are the intervals your ear was trained on before anyone invented theory. Here's what a single note already contains:

harmonic   frequency    interval above fundamental    folded into one octave
   1          f              0.0 c                        0.0 c
   2         2f           1200.0 c   (octave)              0.0 c   ← same note
   3         3f           1902.0 c                       702.0 c   ← THE FIFTH
   4         4f           2400.0 c   (2 octaves)           0.0 c   ← same note
   5         5f           2786.3 c                       386.3 c   ← major third
   6         6f           3102.0 c                       702.0 c   ← fifth again

Read the last column. Harmonics 2, 4 and 8 are all octaves — useless as generators, they give you nothing new. Harmonic 3 is the first one that lands somewhere genuinely new, at 702 cents, and that is the perfect fifth. Harmonic 5 gives the major third at 386.3 cents, but it arrives later and weaker.

So: the fifth is the simplest interval that isn't octave duplication, and it's physically present inside every note you've ever heard. That's why it became the generator in Greece, in China, and nearly everywhere strings and pipes were used.

This is the same 3:2 established in The Comma & Temperament; here we're using it for a different purpose — not to tune, but to generate.

Reading that table, term by term​

(a) Why "cents" instead of frequencies. Pitch intervals multiply: up an octave is ×2, up two octaves is ×4, not ×3. But our ears perceive them as equal steps. Taking a logarithm converts multiplication into addition, so perceived distance becomes ordinary arithmetic:

cents = 1200 × log2(ratio)

The 1200 is a convention — it makes an equal-tempered semitone come out to a round 100 cents. So log2(3) = 1.585 octaves → 1902 cents for harmonic 3.

(b) Why "fold into one octave." Since an octave is "the same note," we only care about position within an octave. Subtracting whole octaves = taking the remainder mod 1200 cents:

harmonic 3:  1902.0 − 1200 = 702.0 c     (one octave down)
harmonic 6:  3102.0 − 2400 = 702.0 c     (two octaves down — same place)

Harmonics 3 and 6 fold to the same spot because 6 = 3 × 2 — it's the fifth, one octave up. Folding is what turns an infinite climbing series into a finite set of pitch classes.

3. The machine​

Combine the two and you get the whole engine of this section:

1. Start at any note.
2. Go up a fifth (× 3/2).
3. Fold back into the starting octave (subtract 1200 c if you went over).
4. Repeat n times.
5. Sort the results low → high. That's your scale.

In one line, working in cents and taking n notes:

scale(n) = sort( { (i × 701.955) mod 1200 : i = 0, 1, 2, … , n−1 } )

That's it. No aesthetics, no culture, no theory — one interval and a modulo. Run it with n = 5 and you get the pentatonic scale. Run it with n = 7 and you get the major scale. Run it with n = 12 and you get the chromatic set.

4. The obvious question this raises​

If the machine runs forever, why stop at any particular n? Nothing in the rule above says 7 is special. You could take 6 notes, or 9, or 23.

And yet 5 and 7 are the two most widespread scale sizes on Earth, in cultures with no contact with each other. Something must distinguish those values of n from their neighbours.

Something does — and it's checkable in about ten lines of arithmetic. That's the next file.

5. Takeaway​

  • The octave 2:1 is the container — near-universal, but useless as a generator because stacking it gives nothing new.
  • The fifth 3:2 is the generator because it's the first harmonic that isn't an octave duplicate — physically present in every note, not a cultural pick.
  • The machine is: stack fifths, fold into an octave, sort. Cents (1200 × log2) turn multiplying ratios into adding distances; folding (mod 1200) turns an endless series into pitch classes.
  • Nothing so far explains when to stop — which is exactly what makes 5, 7 and 12 interesting.
