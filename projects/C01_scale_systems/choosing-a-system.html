Choosing a System

Six files of derivation come down to one reframe, and it's the same one this site applies to every other structure in music:

A scale system is an index. It makes certain questions cheap to answer by making other relationships invisible. There is no correct scale system, only systems tuned to what you intend to ask.

The circle of fifths is a projection. A tuning is a policy for hiding the comma. A fretboard is an index over pitch. A scale is the same kind of object, one level further down — it decides which notes exist before any of those get a say.

1. The four dials​

Every system in this section is a setting of four independent controls.

1 — The generator. Which interval you stack. The fifth (3:2) gives the familiar 5/7/12 family. Stack thirds instead and different sizes become optimal (19, 31). Use instruments with inharmonic partials and the whole question dissolves, because no interval has a privileged claim.

2 — Size. How many notes before you stop. Constrained from below by needing enough material for tension, and from above by memory and by step sizes shrinking below usefulness — the 23.5-cent floor from file 02.

3 — Evenness. How differentiated the step sizes are. This is the key dial and it's a genuine trade:

uneven  (diatonic, ratio 2.26) → strong landmarks, you always know where you are
                                → but each key is a different shape
even    (12-EDO, ratio 1.00)   → total transposition freedom
                                → but no landmarks; can't locate yourself

4 — Symmetry. Whether the set maps onto itself under transposition. Symmetry buys ambiguity and smooth movement; asymmetry buys a sense of home. The interval vector measures this directly.

Dials 3 and 4 are why seven and twelve coexist rather than compete: twelve is the even, symmetric supply; seven is the uneven, asymmetric working set carved out of it. You need both. That relationship is Western tonality.

2. Reading the standard scales as index choices​

Once the dials are visible, the common scales stop being a list to memorize and become a menu of deliberate trade-offs:

ScaleMakes cheapPays with
Pentatonicno tritone → nothing clashes; melody over any diatonic chordno tension, so no functional harmony
Diatonicone tritone → keys, cadences, modulationseven modes' worth of bookkeeping
Harmonic minora raised leading tone → strong minor cadencean awkward augmented second in the middle
Whole-toneperfect symmetry → weightless, no gravityno home, no cadence, exhausting at length
Octatonicsymmetry + tritones everywhere → smooth chromatic motionkey identity dissolves
Chromaticevery note availableno note means anything on its own

This is the same table shape as alternate tunings and alternate charts — because it's the same kind of decision.

3. Using this while writing​

The practical payoff is that "which scale" becomes a question with a derivable answer rather than a stylistic guess:

  • Want a melody that can't go wrong over changes? Pentatonic. Its zero tritone count is why it's forgiving — that's not folklore, it's the interval vector.
  • Need a passage to feel suspended, then resolve? Move from a symmetric set (whole-tone, octatonic) into the diatonic. You're switching landmarks off, then back on. The resolution lands because the ear regains its position.
  • Want maximum harmonic pull? Use the tritone deliberately — it's the rarest interval in the scale and therefore the most informative, per file 03.
  • A modal passage sounding directionless? That's usually correct behavior, not a mistake: modes de-emphasize the V–I tritone resolution. Fix it by strengthening a different landmark, not by adding notes.
  • Considering microtonality? File 06 says what you'd gain (31 for near-perfect thirds, 53 for near-perfect everything) and what you'd lose — every prime division destroys the symmetric structures that 12's divisibility provides.

4. What to carry out of this section​

Three things, in descending order of how much they change your thinking.

Seven and twelve are attractors, not laws. They're the first two stable resting points of stacking fifths and folding into octaves. Reachable by accident, confirmed by independent cultures, explained by mathematics only afterwards.

Seven is primary; twelve is derived. Twelve is the closure of the seven-note scale under transposition — the supply cabinet, not the scale. Western notation still says so, with its seven letters and five borrowed names.

Every layer is the same kind of compromise. Which notes exist (here), how they relate (H01), how to fit them on a real instrument (C02), and how the layout shapes the player's mind (mindsets) — four levels of the same recurring move: make the frequent question cheap, let the rare one get expensive, and forget you ever chose.

5. Takeaway​

  • A scale system is an index: it decides which notes exist, before tuning, charts or fretboards get a vote.
  • Four dials: generator, size, evenness, symmetry. Every system in this section is one setting of them.
  • Evenness is the core trade — landmarks vs transposition freedom. Seven and twelve are the two ends, kept simultaneously; that pairing is tonality.
  • Standard scales read as a menu of trade-offs: pentatonic buys safety with tension, symmetric scales buy weightlessness with home, chromatic buys everything with meaning.
  • Nothing here is a law. Seven and twelve are frozen accidents with good reasons — which is the most useful thing to know about them.
