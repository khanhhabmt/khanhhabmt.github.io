Why Seven Carries Tonality

The last file showed that 5, 7, 12 are the sizes where the fifth-chain produces a clean two-step pattern. That explains why they're learnable. It doesn't explain why seven became the carrier of Western harmony while five stayed melodic and twelve became raw material.

The answer is a second property, independent of step sizes, and it's the reason functional harmony works at all.

1. The problem tonality has to solve​

For a scale to support a key — a sense of "home," of tension and resolution — you need to be able to tell where you are from what you hear. If two different places in the scale sound identical, the ear can't locate itself, and there's no "home" to return to.

So the question becomes: does the diatonic set give every position a unique acoustic address?

2. The interval vector​

The standard tool. Take a set of pitch classes, look at every pair, measure the distance, and tally how often each distance occurs.

diatonic set   C D E F G A B  =  {0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11}
                                  7 notes → 7×6/2 = 21 pairs

interval class:    1    2    3    4    5    6
count:             2    5    4    3    6    1
                   ↑                        ↑
              2 semitones apart        tritone: ONE pair only

Every count is different: 2, 5, 4, 3, 6, 1. Each interval class appears a unique number of times. A scale with this property is called a deep scale, and very few sets have it.

Reading that, term by term​

(a) What an "interval class" is. Distance measured so that inversion doesn't matter. C up to A is 9 semitones; A up to C is 3. Musically these are the same relationship seen from two sides, so we always take the smaller:

interval class = min(d, 12 − d)     where d = (b − a) mod 12

That's why classes run 1–6 and stop: 6 semitones (the tritone) is its own inversion, the maximum possible.

(b) Why counting pairs is the right test. Two positions in a scale are distinguishable if the pattern of intervals around them differs. Tallying all 21 pairs is the compact summary of that pattern. If two interval classes had the same count, the scale would contain a symmetry — some rotation mapping it onto itself — and that symmetry is exactly what destroys a sense of location.

(c) Why "all counts distinct" is the payoff. Because each class has its own frequency, hearing an interval tells you something specific about where you are. The rarer it is, the more it pins down.

3. The one tritone does the heavy lifting​

Look at the rarest entry: interval class 6 appears exactly once. In C major, there is precisely one pair of notes a tritone apart:

the only tritone in C major:   F — B

This single fact is load-bearing for the whole tonal system:

  • It identifies the key. F–B occurs in C major and nowhere else among the twelve major keys. Sound that tritone and you have named a key uniquely — no other information required.
  • It's the engine of the dominant. F and B are the seventh and third of G7, the dominant chord. Its urge to resolve — B → C upward, F → E downward — is the tritone contracting/expanding to the tonic third. That pull is functional harmony, and it exists because the interval is rare and unstable.
  • It makes modulation legible. Change which tritone is sounding and you've changed key, which is why the tritone is the hinge in the modulation mechanics in Circle of Fifths → Modulation.

The leading tone, the dominant seventh, the V–I cadence — none of these are conventions. They're consequences of the diatonic set having exactly one tritone.

4. The control group: scales built to have no landmarks​

The clearest way to see what the diatonic set is doing is to look at scales that deliberately fail the test:

ScaleInterval vector
Diatonic C D E F G A B[2, 5, 4, 3, 6, 1]all distinct — deep scale
Pentatonic C D E G A[0, 3, 2, 1, 4, 0]nearly distinct (two classes simply absent)
Harmonic minor[3, 3, 5, 4, 4, 2]repeats
Whole-tone[0, 6, 0, 6, 0, 3]heavily symmetric
Octatonic (dim)[4, 4, 8, 4, 4, 4]almost totally uniform

The bottom two are the interesting ones. Whole-tone and octatonic are so symmetric that every position sounds like every other position. Transpose a whole-tone scale up a whole step and you get the same set of notes back. There is no home, no leading tone, no cadence — structurally, not stylistically.

And that is exactly why Debussy, Ravel and Messiaen reached for them. Those scales are the standard tool for making music feel weightless, floating, unanchored — dream sequences, water, stasis. Composers use the missing landmarks on purpose. Same property as the diatonic's, run in reverse.

The pentatonic sits in between: no tritone at all (class 6 = 0), so nothing pulls hard anywhere. That's why it's near-impossible to play a "wrong" note on it, why it's the world's default folk and beginner scale, and why it's melodic rather than harmonic. No tension means no functional harmony — and also no way to sound bad.

5. Seven is where both properties meet​

Stack the two requirements and the field collapses to one candidate:

two step sizes (learnable)     →  n = 5, 7, 12
all interval counts distinct   →  the 7-note diatonic set

Five is learnable but has no tritone, so it can't generate tension. Twelve is learnable but perfectly symmetric — every note identical, no landmarks at all (the same failure as whole-tone, taken to the limit). Seven is the only size where the scale is both easy to internalize and rich enough to locate yourself in.

That's not a claim that seven-note scales are better music. It's a claim that if you want functional harmony — keys, cadences, modulation, the entire apparatus of H01 — seven is where the raw material for it appears.

6. Takeaway​

  • Tonality needs positional uniqueness: you must be able to hear where you are in the scale.
  • The diatonic set is a deep scale — interval vector [2,5,4,3,6,1], every count distinct — so every interval is a unique address.
  • Its single tritone (F–B in C major) identifies the key on its own and drives the dominant's pull. Leading tone, V–I, and modulation all follow from that uniqueness.
  • Symmetric scales (whole-tone [0,6,0,6,0,3], octatonic [4,4,8,4,4,4]) have no landmarks — which is precisely why Impressionists use them to remove gravity.
  • Pentatonic has no tritone at all: no tension, no wrong notes, no functional harmony.
  • Seven is the only size satisfying both learnability and uniqueness.
